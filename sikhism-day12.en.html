Sikhism 锡克教

Day 12Dharmic Traditions~26–30 million adherents

On the plains of Punjab — a land cut deep by caste and by the frontier between Hinduism and Islam — a teacher said something that in his day bordered on scandal: "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim." He was not dismissing either faith, but pointing to something more basic: before the one God, a person has only one identity. From that seed grew Sikhism — among the youngest of the world's major religions, barely five centuries old, yet one that fused strict monotheism, radical equality, and a this-worldly discipline into a path all its own.

"In all light shines that one same Light."—— Guru Granth Sahib, p. 663 — the Sikh expression of God's presence pervading all things

History & Origins

Ten Gurus, one scripture, one community

Sikhism arose in the late 15th century in Punjab (now straddling the India–Pakistan border). Its founder, Guru Nanak (1469–1539) — "Guru" means a teacher who dispels darkness — was born into a Hindu family, but through travel and meditation formed his own message: there is only one God, formless and beyond the divisions of Hindu and Muslim; all people are equal before that God, unseparated by caste, gender, or birth.

Nine further Gurus followed in unbroken succession, shaping the teaching from a circle of followers into a community with a scripture, holy sites, and institutions. A few turning points: the fifth, Guru Arjan, compiled the first scripture, the Adi Granth, in 1604, and built at Amritsar the central shrine later known as the Golden Temple; he was executed in 1606, the first Guru to be martyred. The sixth, Guru Hargobind, articulated miri-piri ("two swords," temporal and spiritual), and the community took up both devotion and self-defense. The ninth, Guru Tegh Bahadur, was put to death in Delhi in 1675 by the Mughal state for refusing to convert while defending others' freedom of belief (by tradition, Kashmiri Hindus).

The tenth and last living Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, did two things that set the face of Sikhism. In 1699 he founded the Khalsa ("the pure"), a body of committed Sikhs bound by vows and shared marks; and before his death in 1708 he declared that there would be no further living Guru — henceforth the eternal Guru would be the scripture itself, thereafter honoured as the Guru Granth Sahib, the living authority of a faith that takes "the Word as its teacher."

Where It Stands Today

Highly concentrated, and highly dispersed

Sikhism is the world's fifth-largest organized religion, with roughly 26–30 million adherents (estimates vary with source and method), about 0.4% of humanity. Around 90% (some 24 million) live in India, and Indian Sikhs are in turn heavily concentrated in the northwestern state of Punjab — home to over 14 million Sikhs, more than half its population, the only administrative region on earth with a Sikh majority.

At the same time, Sikhism is among the most classically diasporic communities. Since the 19th century, Sikhs have migrated worldwide as soldiers, farmers, artisans, and traders; today there are sizable populations in the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States — Canada, at about 2% Sikh, has the highest national share. This pattern of "a dense homeland, a scattered diaspora" makes the turbaned, bearded Sikh man a familiar figure in cities across the globe. Sikhism is largely a tradition of descent with universal ideals: doctrinally it proclaims the equality of all and welcomes everyone, yet in practice it is carried mainly by Punjabi families across generations, with little active proselytizing.

Core Beliefs

One formless God, one this-worldly discipline

Sikhism is firmly monotheist. Its faith is distilled into the opening symbol of its scripture — Ik Onkar, "One God": a single Creator, formless and without image, without beginning or end, both beyond all things and pervading them all; Sikhs often invoke this God as Waheguru, the "Wondrous Lord." This God has no gender, never became human, and cannot be depicted in an idol — so Sikhism has no idols and no priestly caste, and holds that no caste, ritual, or self-mortification can of itself lead to God.

It shares the vocabulary of karma and rebirth with the Indian traditions: people are bound to the cycle of birth and death by attachment and self-centeredness (haumai). But the way out lies not in renunciation but in Naam Simran — the constant remembrance and inward realization of God's presence within ordinary life, so that, by grace, the small self merges into the divine. Sikhism explicitly rejects world-renouncing asceticism: the ideal seeker is a householder, "in the world but not of it," working honestly, supporting a family, doing good, while keeping the mind fixed on God. Principles & Practice

Turning equality into a shared meal

Sikhism made the abstract ideal of human equality strikingly concrete. In its central space, the gurdwara ("door to the Guru"), nearly every practice enacts the same thing — that before God and one another, there is no high or low:

  • The langar (community kitchen): every gurdwara runs a free vegetarian kitchen open to anyone, regardless of religion, caste, wealth, or gender, where all sit together on the floor and eat from one pot. The institution is traced to Guru Nanak and was meant to break, in public, the purity hierarchy of caste — the langar at Amritsar's Golden Temple is said to serve well over 100,000 meals a day.
  • Scripture as teacher: worship centres on the reading and singing of the Guru Granth Sahib, honoured as the living Guru — attended morning and night, before a congregation seated barefoot with covered heads. Sikhism has no ordained clergy: any qualified man or woman may lead worship and read the scripture.
  • A common name and marks: those initiated into the Khalsa keep the Five Ks — uncut hair (Kesh, often bound in a turban), a wooden comb (Kangha), a steel bangle (Kara), a short sword (Kirpan), and cotton shorts (Kachera) — and commonly take the names Singh ("lion") and Kaur ("princess") to shed the caste marking carried by surnames. Abstaining from tobacco and alcohol, and not cutting the hair, are common observances.
  • The rhythm of life: major festivals include the Gurpurab marking Guru Nanak's birth and the spring festival of Vaisakhi commemorating the Khalsa's founding; life-cycle rites (naming, initiation, the Anand Karaj wedding, funerals) all centre on the scripture. Not every Sikh is initiated — many keep uncut hair and a turban without formally entering the Khalsa, and others do not; devotion and form vary widely.

Common Misreadings

Misreading: Sikhism is a blend or branch of Hinduism and Islam.
It did arise where the two met in Punjab, and it dialogued with Hindu devotional movements and Islamic Sufism — but Sikhs regard it as a complete tradition revealed through the Gurus: its own monotheism, its own scripture, its own shrines and rites, accepting neither the Vedas nor the Qur'an as final authority. It is not a synthesis of anything older.

Misreading: a turbaned, bearded man must be Muslim (or Arab).
This is a widespread misidentification. The turban (dastar) and uncut hair and beard are marks of Sikhism, not Islam, and turbaned Sikhs are mostly of Punjabi descent. Such misrecognition has, in places, led to harm against Sikhs, and is worth correcting.

Misreading: carrying a kirpan means this is a warlike, aggressive religion.
The short sword is one of the Five Ks — a religious article symbolizing the duty to defend justice and protect the weak, alongside self-restraint, not a tool of attack; teaching restricts its use to defending the innocent. The tradition holds "two swords," temporal and spiritual, yet places service, generosity, and equality at its core.

Misreading: all Sikhs are initiated and wear the turban and Five Ks.
Those baptized into the Khalsa and keeping the full Five Ks (amritdhari) are one part of the community; many others keep uncut hair and a turban without formal initiation, and some do not. Sikh identity is a spectrum of forms and degrees of observance.

Cross-Tradition Reading · Contemporary Situation

A bridge across two families. On this site's map, Sikhism is a rare faith that grows between two sets of traditions at once: its one formless God and rejection of idols echo the Abrahamic faiths (Days 1–8), while its idiom of karma, rebirth, and liberation shares a root with the Indian traditions (Days 9–11). Yet it belongs to neither — Nanak's "there is no Hindu, there is no Muslim" was precisely a step past the existing doors, toward a new path framed by "one God + radical equality + this-worldly labour." Read alongside Judaism's monotheism and Jainism's doctrine of karma, it shows most clearly how, on the map of human belief, traditions can be neighbours and still be nothing alike.

An old idea's modern echo. The langar — all people seated and fed together, whatever their standing — is rediscovered today by many as a plain but powerful practice of equality; Sikh communities worldwide are known for large-scale free meals in disasters and crises (mobile langars in many places), turning ancient teaching into contemporary public service.

Tensions today. Two clusters of issues move the contemporary Sikh world: one is sustaining diaspora identity — how second- and third-generation Sikhs abroad hold the turban and the disciplines while integrating locally, and how they face bias born of being misidentified by appearance; the other is the historical memory of Punjab — a series of late-20th-century conflicts around Sikh identity and politics that remain a sensitive subject, to be handled carefully and analytically both within the community and in Indian politics. This page offers only a historical, structural account and endorses no side.

Going Deeper

Born in India and sharing the vocabulary of karma and rebirth, why does Sikhism insist it is a strict monotheism rather than a branch of Hinduism?
The pivot is that "God" is redefined. Hinduism embraces many deities and avatars, and also an impersonal ultimate reality (Brahman); Sikhism affirms only a single formless God who never incarnates and cannot be depicted in an idol, and on that basis rejects idol worship, priestly mediation, and caste purity — precisely the practices where it parts from Hindu society. It borrows karma and rebirth to describe the human predicament, but swaps the hinge of liberation to "remembering the one God by grace," rather than fulfilling caste duty or elaborate ritual. Shared vocabulary is not a shared framework: the same "karma–rebirth–liberation" language is set inside a new, monotheist, anti-hierarchical structure. That is why Sikhs insist it is an independent revelation, not a reformed Hinduism.
Sikhism speaks of "two swords" and wears a kirpan, yet upholds nonviolence and service — how do these coexist?
The tradition does not classify itself as absolute pacifism, but as a conditional force for justice. The sixth Guru's miri-piri (wearing both a worldly and a spiritual sword) held that faith should not be worldless and powerless, but able to protect the weak and resist oppression. The kirpan thus symbolizes "responsibility and restraint in guarding justice"; teaching strictly limits its use to defending the innocent once peaceful means are exhausted — the weight is on defense and self-discipline, not aggression. Set beside this are the daily practices of service (seva), generosity, and equality. Sikhism, in effect, tries to hold a difficult balance: neither so meek as to permit injustice, nor letting strength slide into aggression. That very tension is part of its ethics.
What is the difference between honouring a scripture as the "eternal living Guru" and treating it as a holy book?
The difference is personhood. Most traditions treat scripture as a sacred text — a record of revelation to be studied and obeyed. Sikhism, by a singular arrangement of the tenth Guru in 1708, held that there would be no further Guru in flesh: the Guru's authority and presence rest directly in the scripture itself. So the Guru Granth Sahib is attended as a living Guru — with its own seat and canopy, opened at dawn and put to rest at night, before a congregation with heads covered and feet bare. This both solves the succession problem nearly every religion faces (authority no longer attaches to a person or class, and so is harder to usurp or split) and anchors the final teaching lineage firmly in a public, recitable, fixed text. Scripture becomes not merely an object of reading but a teacher treated as present.
