Confucianism

Day 14East Asian Traditionshundreds of thousands who say so; hundreds of millions who do it

The hardest question about Confucianism is whether it is a religion at all. There is no creator god, no church, no rite of entry; Confucius "did not speak of prodigies, feats of strength, disorder or spirits," and said "not yet understanding life, how could you understand death?" And yet it has Heaven, it has the Mandate of Heaven, and it has a body of sacrificial rites unbroken for more than two thousand years, rites that set the rhythm of East Asian life from funerals to civil service. The argument is still live — and what is usually being argued over is not Confucianism but the word "religion."

"Sacrifice as if present; sacrifice to the spirits as if the spirits were present."— Analects 3.12

History & Origins

A founder who claimed to found nothing

Confucius (551–479 BCE) was born in the state of Lu and described himself as "a transmitter and not a maker, trusting in and loving the ancient ways." He did not set out to found a religion; he set out to reinterpret the ritual and musical tradition of the Zhou at a moment when it was visibly collapsing. The Analects is a record compiled by disciples and later followers, not a book he wrote. Mencius then argued that human nature is good and Xunzi that it is bad and that ritual is a human construction — the tradition was never a single block.

The turn came under the Han. In 136 BCE erudite chairs were established for the Five Classics, and in 124 BCE an imperial academy; Dong Zhongshu wired yin-yang cosmology and portent theory into Confucian learning as "resonance between Heaven and humanity." Later shorthand calls this "dismissing the hundred schools and honouring Confucian learning alone," but recent decades of scholarship have qualified that heavily: the ascendancy was gradual, only settling in the Eastern Han, and early Han governing practice was far from purely Confucian.

Two institutions gave Confucianism its properly religious shape. One was the temple-school: every state school had a Confucian temple attached where the shidian rite was performed — roughly 1,500 of them across China by the late Qing. The other was the examination system. In 1313 the Yuan court made Zhu Xi's commentaries on the Four Books the sole orthodox standard, and for nearly six centuries every candidate memorised the same annotations, until the exams were abolished in 1905.

Neo-Confucianism was the second reconstruction: Zhu Xi (1130–1200) rebuilt a metaphysics out of principle, vital force, mind and nature — both answering and absorbing Buddhist and Daoist thought — while Wang Yangming opened a rival "school of mind."

Transmission abroad followed the same rite-school-state channel: Vietnam built the Temple of Literature in Hanoi in 1070; the Joseon dynasty (from 1392) governed Korea on Confucian lines through the Seonggyungwan and a network of local schools; in 1690 the Tokugawa shogunate built the Yushima Seidō in Edo, headed by the Hayashi family.

The twentieth century broke the frame. Abolishing the examinations in 1905 removed the institution that carried it; the New Culture Movement called for "smashing the Confucius shop"; during the "Criticise Lin Biao, Criticise Confucius" campaign of 1973–74 the temple and cemetery at Qufu were vandalised. The turn came in the 1980s — New Confucian scholars in Hong Kong and Taiwan had already issued a manifesto on Chinese culture in 1958; in 2004 Qufu resumed an official public ceremony, and the rite entered China's first national list of intangible cultural heritage in 2006.

Where It Stands Today

The smallest count, the widest reach

"How many Confucians are there" is a question whose answer runs from a hundred thousand to a billion, depending on how you ask.

  • Narrow (people who give "Confucianism" as their religion): very few. The 2015 South Korean census recorded 0.2%, about a hundred thousand people; the World Religion Database's global figure for Confucianists is likewise in the low millions.
  • The institutional exception: Indonesia is the only country that lists Confucianism (Khonghucu) among its official religions — recognised in 1965, withdrawn under the New Order, restored after 2000. In the 1971 census 0.7% of the population registered as Confucian; by 2022 the figure was about 75,000 people, or 0.03%. The collapse largely reflects the years when adherents were obliged to register as something else.
  • Broad (counting those who venerate ancestors and organise family life around filial duty and rite): hundreds of millions.

A Korean study lays the gap out plainly: 0.2% on the census, but when asked "regardless of your religious affiliation, do you consider yourself a Confucian," 51% said yes; take performing ancestral rites at a gravesite as the criterion and the share reaches 86%. Same population, three numbers.

Mainland China is similar. Confucianism is not among the five religions the state officially recognises, and there is no membership or clergy system. But the Pew Research Center's 2023 report Measuring Religion in China, drawing on the 2018 Chinese General Social Survey, found that about three-quarters of adults had visited a family member's grave in the past year — 75% of the religiously unaffiliated and 70% of the affiliated, which is very nearly the same.

Geographically it covers mainland China and Taiwan, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, Singapore and Chinese communities worldwide; Confucian temples are estimated at over three thousand globally. It does not proselytise and has no mechanism of conversion, passing instead through family upbringing and schooling. The past two decades have seen a grassroots revival in China — academies, classics-recitation, "village Confucianism" — alongside an unresolved scholarly argument about whether Confucianism should be institutionalised as a religion at all.

Core Beliefs

Heaven above, the person inside human relations

Heaven is not a creator

"Heaven" has will and moral character, and warns through anomalies and disasters (the Han doctrine of resonance); in Neo-Confucianism it is recast as "the principle of Heaven," an impersonal order. But Heaven does not create the world, does not reveal itself, does not become incarnate, and does not accept transactional petition — "one who offends against Heaven has no one to pray to." The Mandate of Heaven covers both a dynasty's legitimacy and the lot allotted to each person.

Ren and li

Ren is the fellow feeling between persons; li, ritual propriety, is the form that lets it land. One influential twentieth-century reading (Fingarette, 1972) argued that ritual itself carries the sacred: no other shore is required, and the ordinary courtesies, done properly, are where the holy actually is.

Not salvation but becoming a person

The endpoint is not being saved after death but becoming a sage — making oneself, in this world, into a complete human being. The Great Learning sets out the path: investigating things, extending knowledge, making the intention sincere, rectifying the mind, cultivating the self, ordering the family, governing the state, bringing peace to all under Heaven. The afterlife gets almost no attention; "revere the spirits but keep them at a distance" is a statement about proportion, not about whether they exist.

Canon and internal disagreement

The Five Classics (Changes, Documents, Odes, Rites, Spring and Autumn Annals) and the Four Books (Great Learning, Doctrine of the Mean, Analects, Mencius) — Zhu Xi placed the Four Books ahead of the Five Classics, and they became the entry canon thereafter. The disagreements never went away: Mencius on the goodness of human nature against Xunzi on its badness, Zhu Xi's school of principle against Wang Yangming's school of mind, Qing evidential scholarship against Song metaphysics.

Principles & Practice

The table at home, the rite at the temple

  • Ancestral rites: the largest living Confucian practice. Spirit tablets, death-day offerings, grave-sweeping at Qingming and the Ghost Festival; Korea has charye and gijesa, and every Vietnamese household has its ancestral altar. The four rites codified in Zhu Xi's Family Rituals (12th century) — capping, marriage, mourning, sacrifice — remain the template for East Asian funerals today.
  • Honouring Confucius: the shidian. The rite is still performed at Qufu, at the Taipei Confucius Temple, at the Munmyo shrine of Korea's Sungkyunkwan (twice yearly), and at the Yushima Seidō in Tokyo. The title matters: within the rite Confucius is the First Teacher, receiving offerings but not granting favours. This is requital, not a request for efficacy.
  • Rites of state. Emperors historically performed the great sacrifice to Heaven. Korea's Jongmyo royal ancestral rite and its ritual music were inscribed on a UNESCO list in 2001 and continue today with the standing of cultural heritage.

Ethics on the axis of filial duty

The five relations (parent-child, ruler-subject, husband-wife, elder-younger, friend-friend) turn on filial duty: supporting the living and sacrificing to the dead are two ends of one obligation. As for the rigid "three bonds," that hardening came after the Han and tracked the needs of imperial rule; the early texts also contain "follow the Way, not the ruler" and Mencius's "the people are the most valuable." Both sides belong to the tradition.

Study as cultivation

Reading, academies, household precepts, genealogies — treating study itself as self-cultivation is the tradition's most distinctive practice. There is no priesthood; the teacher and the scholar carry something close to that role.

Adjustments now under way

Korea has seen eldest daughters take the officiant's role and rites simplified; Chinese families are shortening ceremonies and moving ancestors into columbaria. Who may officiate, and where women stand in the rites, is the liveliest argument inside the tradition today.

Common Misconceptions

Misconception: it is only ethics or philosophy, not a religion.
It has Heaven and the Mandate, sacrificial rites, a canon and ritual specialists — it simply builds no separate church. The sociologist C. K. Yang called this a "diffused religion" in 1961: rather than setting up its own institution, it spreads through the family, the school and the state. Other scholars insist it is humanistic cultivation rather than religion. The Chinese debate over whether Confucianism is a religion, running since 1978, has produced no consensus.

Misconception: Confucianism just means hierarchy and the suppression of the individual.
Institutionalised Confucianism did underwrite a hierarchical order for centuries, and there is no need to deny it. But equating one imperial-era form with the whole tradition loses its internal resources for criticism — the scholar-official's duty to hold the Way against power comes from the same source.

Misconception: Confucius is worshipped as a god.
In the rite his title is First Teacher; he receives offerings but does not dispense fortune. Honorifics accumulated over the dynasties, yet in the mainstream ritual system he is not a deity one petitions. Popular practice does sometimes treat him as a patron of examinations, but that has already stepped outside the ritual code.

Misconception: Confucianism is anti-religious, or atheist.
"Revere the spirits but keep them at a distance" is about proportion and not overstepping, not denial; "sacrifice as if present" brackets the metaphysical question and attends instead to the disposition of the person sacrificing. Historically it was entirely normal for a Confucian scholar to visit Buddhist temples and hire Daoist priests for a funeral.

Bridges & the Contemporary Scene

The three teachings divide labour; they are not a choice of one. The East Asian norm is for one person to conduct themselves by Confucian norms, seek health through Daoism, and hold a Buddhist funeral (Day 13 covered Daoism; Day 16 takes up the wider folk ecology). Confucianism occupies the slot marked public order and family ethics.

An echo of Judaism. Both are heavily oriented to this world, to family and generational continuity, with little said about the hereafter, and both put holiness inside concrete daily rules (Day 1). The divergence is just as clear: one has a covenanting personal God who reveals; the Confucian Heaven neither covenants nor reveals.

A revealing episode. The Chinese Rites Controversy of the 17th–18th centuries turned on exactly this question — whether the rites to ancestors and to Confucius counted as religious worship. Jesuits held they were civil custom; other orders held they were idolatry. Rome banned them from 1704, reaffirmed the ban and forbade further debate in 1742, and only in 1939 permitted Chinese Catholics to take part in ancestral and Confucian ceremonies. The same acts, judged as two different kinds of thing across two centuries — this issue's question in historical form.

The measuring stick. "Religion" as a modern category was shaped by Christian institutional experience: creed, church, exclusive belonging. Applied to a tradition that builds no church and demands no exclusivity, it was almost bound to misread (Days 21 and 25 return to this).

Tensions today. Proposals to make Confucianism a state religion, or to build a "Confucian constitutionalism," draw both support and sharp criticism in Chinese-language scholarship; the thesis that Confucian ethics powered East Asia's economic take-off remains empirically contested — the same ethic was once invoked to explain stagnation and later to explain growth.

Going Deeper

With no creator god and no promised afterlife, what carries the religious load?
Two things take the weight. First, sacrifice: the ancestors are not on a far shore but on the other side of the offering table, and the relation between living and dead is maintained by repeated rite — so ultimate concern lands on lineage continuing and on not being forgotten. Second, self-cultivation: the endpoint is not rescue but completion, and meaning comes from how fully one has made oneself a person. The suggestion is that settling death and supplying order and meaning need not be carried by a transcendent deity. Whether that arrangement suffices is a judgement each reader makes — and it is precisely the question the tradition has been asked for two millennia.
Can rite substitute for belief? What kind of community forms when orthodoxy is defined by conduct rather than creed?
In creedal traditions the boundary is drawn by what you believe, which calls for creeds and for machinery to police heresy. In a ritual tradition the boundary is drawn by what you do — whoever performs the rites is inside, and what they privately think goes largely unexamined. That explains why Confucianism developed almost nothing resembling an inquisition, and why it could coexist with Buddhism and Daoism without having to expel anyone. The cost sits on the other side: when orthodoxy is behavioural, pressure shifts onto the strict execution of everyday propriety. Worth reading against Islamic law and Jewish mitzvot — also practice-centred traditions, with quite different boundary mechanisms.
When you measure Confucianism with "religion," are you measuring Confucianism or the ruler?
The value of this argument may lie less in a final verdict than in what it exposes about the instrument. The modern concept of religion carries identifiable assumptions: single affiliation, explicit creed, independent church, a transcendent god. What fits is a religion; what does not is called philosophy or culture — a taxonomy that keeps failing in East Asia, and that shaped colonial-era religious policy and still shapes census design. The more interesting inversion: if you built a concept out of East Asian experience first and then applied it to Christianity, what would you measure?

This piece introduces the Confucian tradition from a neutral, comparative standpoint, presenting its internal logic and diversity without praise, blame or ranking. Whether Confucianism is a religion remains unsettled in scholarship; the main positions are set out side by side. Population figures are recent estimates and vary enormously with the definition of religious identity.
