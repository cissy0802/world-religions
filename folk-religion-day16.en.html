East Asian Folk Religion

Day 16East Asian traditionsHundreds of millions · counts vary wildly (see below)

These are the hardest people in the world to count. Ask the same population a different question and they shift from "1.3 billion with no religion" to "incense in every home, a temple on every corner." East Asian folk religion has no founder, no canon, no rite of entry — not even a name it claims for itself. It is not a fourth teaching alongside the three; it is the shared foundation the three are built on.

"Sacrifice is offered to those who gave laws to the people, who died in the discharge of duty, who stabilized the state by their labor, who warded off great calamity, who averted great disaster."— Book of Rites, "Laws of Sacrifice." A two-thousand-year-old test for who qualifies as a god: not birth, but service rendered

Origins & History

No starting point, only sediment

Folk religion has no founding moment, only layers that keep thickening. The bottom stratum is the Shang–Zhou practice of ancestral sacrifice alongside offerings to the soil, the grain, the mountains and rivers: each family with its ancestors, each village with its earth god, each state with its altars.

Once Buddhism arrived and Daoism took institutional shape, the phrase "the Three Teachings" came into use in the Six Dynasties. But for most people it was never a matter of choosing among the three — it was a matter of using all of them: whoever solves the problem gets the visit.

The Song dynasty was the hinge. Cities and commerce expanded, local gods multiplied, and the court began granting plaques and titles that folded influential local deities into the state register of sacrifices — Mazu was promoted across dynasties from "Lady" to "Consort" to "Heavenly Consort," and under the Qing to "Empress of Heaven." It was a trade: the local god gained legitimacy, the state gained a channel into village life. What this actually changed is still debated: one school holds that imperial titles produced a real standardization of South China's gods (James Watson); another argues that localities often simply adopted the official label while carrying on as before — "pseudo-standardization" (Michael Szonyi).

The Ming and Qing pushed institutionalization further: an edict of 1370 reorganized City God temples nationwide by prefecture, department and county, while Guan Yu's honorific titles accumulated from the Song onward until, by the late Qing, the full string ran to twenty-six characters. "Unity of the Three Teachings" became a popular slogan, and the Fujianese Lin Zhao'en (1517–1598) founded an actual Three-in-One teaching on that principle.

The twentieth century brought a new set of categories. The word "religion" entered Chinese via the Japanese coinage zongjiao, and with it came the paired category of "superstition": what has doctrine, clergy and canon counts as religion, the rest does not. In 1928 the Nationalist government issued Standards for Preserving or Abolishing Shrines, sorting shrines into "sages," "religion," "ancient gods" and "illicit cults" to decide which would survive. Different places then took different paths through suppression and recovery; incense burned again from the 1980s, and since the 2000s the tradition has acquired one more identity — intangible cultural heritage (Mazu belief and customs inscribed by UNESCO in 2009, Vietnam's Mother Goddess worship in 2016).

Distribution Today

One population, two numbers

Start with two counts an order of magnitude apart. In 2012 the Pew Research Center estimated some 405 million "folk religionists" worldwide, about 6% of humanity. After Pew revised its method for estimating religion in China in 2025, roughly 1.3 billion people in China (about 90%) were counted as religiously unaffiliated, and folk religion no longer appears as a top-level category at all. Same people, two counts — the difference is not in them but in the question.

Ask about behavior and the picture inverts:

  • Mainland China: in the 2018 China Family Panel Studies, 33% believed in the Buddha or bodhisattvas, 18% in Daoist immortals, and 47% in fengshui; in the 2018 Chinese General Social Survey, about three-quarters had visited a family gravesite in the past year.
  • Five East Asian societies: Pew surveyed more than 10,000 adults in 2023 (Vietnam, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Japan, South Korea). In Vietnam 96% had burned incense for ancestors in the past year and nearly every household keeps an altar; in Taiwan and Japan roughly eight in ten had burned incense; in Taiwan, Japan and Vietnam about half or more say mountains, rivers or trees have spirits.
  • Taiwan: about 12,000 temples were registered with the Ministry of the Interior as of 2019 (roughly 80% filed as Daoist); in an Academia Sinica survey the same year, about 49% identified with "folk belief."

On the mainland, "folk belief" is administered as a category of its own: a 2002 province-wide survey in Fujian counted over 30,000 substantial temples, and Zhejiang has been registering and numbering folk-belief sites one by one since 2015. The definitions differ, the order of magnitude does not: it dwarfs any single registered religion.

The densest belt runs from the Fujian–Guangdong coast through Taiwan into the Chinese communities of Southeast Asia; around it lie Vietnam's Mother Goddess worship and ancestor rites, the Ryukyuan noro priestesses and yuta spirit-mediums, and Korean musok shamanism (no reliable count of active shamans exists; estimates run from tens of thousands to well over a hundred thousand). It does not proselytize; it travels with people. The temple follows the migrant, rather than the missionary carrying the temple.

Core Beliefs

No doctrine, but a tight logic

The gods are a bureaucracy

The most conspicuous structural feature is a chain of divine command: the Jade Emperor above; the Great Emperor of Mount Tai and the graded City Gods holding the registers of life and death for each jurisdiction; the Earth God running a single village; the Stove God stationed in every household kitchen. The paperwork and the personal-favor logic of imperial administration transfer wholesale — filing a petition, burning spirit money, lodging a complaint before the City God all have real-world originals. Divine rank can rise and fall: gods who deliver get promoted, gods who stop delivering are left to fade.

Efficacy is the currency

The test is not doctrinal correctness but whether it works (ling). A god that works draws incense, temple expansions and daughter shrines; a god that stops working is abandoned. This efficacy standard is why the system cannot generate an orthodoxy — there is no body to declare heresy, because the verdict rests with every individual holding a stick of incense.

Gods, ghosts and ancestors

The anthropologist Arthur Wolf's classic 1974 analysis identified a three-way structure: the dead who receive offerings from descendants are ancestors (kin); the unmourned dead with no line to feed them are ghosts (strangers, beggars); those who served the community and received titles and sacrifices are gods (officials). The difference lies not in the dead person but in the relationship: your ancestor is my ghost. The seventh-month Universal Salvation rites are precisely a public provision for other people's ghosts.

The shared substrate

Qi, yin and yang, fate, retribution and fengshui form a cosmology all three teachings draw on. "Unity of the Three Teachings" is not a merger: at the user's end it looks like a division of labor — temples and Daoist priests for festivals and god-birthdays, Buddhist monks for the seven-week funerary cycle, a Confucian frame for lineage ethics and the ancestral tablets. One person may use all three in a single year without feeling any need to choose.

Principles & Practice

You don't join; you do things

There is no initiation, no membership roll, no creed to recite. Participation is physical: burning incense; casting moon blocks (two crescent blocks thrown to put a yes-or-no question to the god — one up and one down means yes, both flat sides up means the question was badly put, both rounded sides up means no); drawing and interpreting oracle sticks; making a vow and returning to redeem it; lighting a lamp for the year.

The yearly rhythm

  • Calendar festivals: sending off and welcoming the gods at New Year, the Lantern Festival, grave-sweeping at Qingming, the Dragon Boat expulsion of pestilence, the seventh-month Universal Salvation feeding of hungry ghosts, Mid-Autumn, the winter solstice.
  • God-birthdays: each temple keeps its patron's day — Mazu's on the 23rd of the third lunar month, Guandi's, the Earth God's. These are the largest mobilizations of the local year.
  • Life-cycle rites: the one-month celebration for a newborn, choosing an auspicious wedding date, the seven-times-seven funerary cycle and merit rituals, the tablet's entry into the ancestral hall and its annual offerings thereafter.

Who officiates

Most temples have no standing clergy. They are run by a management committee and by rotating lay officers — the incense-burner master and his stewards — while ritual specialists are hired as needed: Daoist priests, ritual masters, spirit mediums (through whom a god speaks), geomancers, fortune-tellers. The neighbors work the same way: the Korean shaman who conducts a gut, the Vietnamese medium in a hầu đồng possession ritual, are engaged professionals rather than ordained staff of a church.

Incense division: a temple lineage

A new temple takes a portion of the ash and flame from a mother temple — dividing the incense — and thereafter returns periodically on pilgrimage, producing a network of temples that reads like a genealogy. It is also the technology by which migrants carried their gods to Taiwan and Southeast Asia. Taiwan's Dajia Mazu procession runs nine days and eight nights over some three hundred kilometers, with organizers estimating participants in the hundreds of thousands; such events today are simultaneously ritual, local mobilization and tourism.

Internal variation

Hokkien, Hakka, Teochew, Vietnamese and Ryukyuan communities each keep their own pantheons, and the same god's legends and taboos can differ from village to village with no central body to reconcile them. That is the tradition's fragility and also the reason it survives bans: you can demolish a temple, but not a way of doing things.

Common Misconceptions

Misconception: folk religion is just a downmarket version of Daoism (or Buddhism).
The three have long fed each other: Daoism absorbs popular gods, popular practice borrows Daoist liturgy and Buddhist penitential rites. But folk religion has no independent clergy, hierarchy or canon; the fact that most Taiwanese temples register as Daoist is an administrative convenience, not an organizational membership.

Misconception: it is "superstition," not religion.
The religion/superstition split is a modern category imported in the 19th and 20th centuries, keyed to the presence of doctrine, clergy and canon — the ruler itself has a history. Scholarship treats that distinction as an object of study, not a verdict.

Misconception: worshipping at any temple you pass means the belief is indiscriminate.
The choices follow a clear logic: by function (children, wealth, healing), by track record of efficacy, by ties of place and kinship. A vow must be redeemed and a favor repaid — it is a rule-governed reciprocity, not randomness.

Misconception: surveys show 90% with no religion, so East Asia must be the least religious place on earth.
The surveys ask about affiliation, and this tradition never ran on affiliation. Ask the same people about behavior and the rates of incense, grave-sweeping, fengshui and fortune-telling are very high. Low affiliation is not low religiosity.

Cross-Tradition Bridges · The Contemporary Situation

A mirror to Shinto (Day 15). Both put practice above creed and place above doctrine, and both sit awkwardly inside the modern category of "religion." The difference is organization: Japan has a national corporate body in the Association of Shinto Shrines, while Chinese folk religion never acquired a unified structure — which makes it harder to count and also harder to abolish in one stroke.

Relation to the three teachings (Days 13, 14). It is not a branch of Daoism or Confucianism but the ground all three stand on: Daoism supplies pantheon and liturgy, Buddhism supplies the machinery of the afterlife and karma, Confucianism supplies ancestors and lineage order, and popular practice supplies the use case.

Structurally comparable intermediaries. Catholic and Orthodox intercession of the saints (Days 3, 4) and the spirit systems of African and diaspora traditions (Day 20) invite structural comparison: an intermediary figure standing between the worshipper and a distant high god. But Catholic theology sharply distinguishes veneration of saints from worship of God, a distinction with no counterpart here — structural resemblance is not doctrinal identity.

The contemporary situation. Heritage listing brings funding and tourism revenue, and a tension with it: what gets inscribed is "custom" and "culture," while for practitioners the core has always been efficacy. The protective apparatus preserves the form; how it treats the religious content varies by place. Temples remain organizing nodes of local society — committees, donations, public affairs — and new forms have appeared, from online offerings to QR-code lamp lighting. Governance approaches differ considerably across the region; this page describes the phenomena without evaluating them.

Going Deeper

If a tradition has no name, no clergy and no creed, does "how many adherents" still mean anything?
The unit "adherent" comes from bounded religions: you join, you are registered, you are not something else. Applied to an unbounded practice it yields a meaningless number — either near zero (nobody claims the affiliation) or near everybody. The workable alternative is to measure specific behaviors: shares who swept a grave, burned incense, chose an auspicious date or drew an oracle stick in the past year. What you get is not an adherent count but a map of practice intensity. More generally, any tradition not organized by affiliation will be systematically undercounted in global religious statistics.
The gods are bureaucrats — did religious imagination copy the state, or did the state borrow the gods' legitimacy?
There is evidence in both directions. Divine jurisdictions, ranks and titles do imitate imperial administration, and titles were granted from above. But localities also used gods to underwrite their own order: the temple's boundary was the village's boundary, and a procession route traced the reach of a community. The likelier answer is two-way — the state used gods to reach the grassroots, localities used imperial titles to secure standing, each taking what it needed from one set of symbols. That is also the substance of the Watson–Szonyi dispute: beneath the official name, how much actually changed?
Heritage listing preserves the festival while rewriting it from "efficacy" into "culture." Where is the line between preservation and hollowing out?
Inscription usually requires that a practice be exhibitable and transmissible, so the least translatable parts — possession, taboo, the causal claim that it works — tend to be softened, leaving costumes, routes and music as the visible shell. Yet the counterexamples are real: resources have restarted lapsed festivals, and young people who came back to the temple under a cultural banner ended up practicing. The decisive question may not be the listing itself but who holds the power of definition. If it stays with worshippers and temple committees, the cultural label is only a coat; if it migrates to administrators and the market, the core can be replaced piece by piece.

This page introduces East Asian folk religion from a neutral, comparative standpoint, presenting its internal logic and diversity without praise, blame or ranking. "Folk religion" is used as a descriptive scholarly term, not a value judgment. The figures are recent survey estimates and vary enormously with the method used (self-reported affiliation vs. reported practice; registered sites vs. actual ones); sources and years are noted in the text.
