World Religions

Zoroastrianism

Day 17Ancient & minority traditionsc. 110,000–200,000 adherents

Perhaps a hundred thousand adherents remain worldwide — fewer people than live in a mid-sized town. Yet trace the genealogy of a final judgment, a heaven and a hell, a savior still to come, a last battle in which good defeats evil, and a surprising number of threads run back to this almost-forgotten tradition of the Iranian plateau. Its central claim is not the fire-worship outsiders imagine, either. It is this: the world is good as made, and every person, every day, casts a vote for one side or the other in thought, word and deed.

"Hear with your ears the best things, look upon them with clear-seeing thought, and then each man and woman, for their own self, must choose."— the Gathas, Yasna 30:2, generally taken to be the prophet's own verse

Origins & History

A prophet older than the Persian Empire

The prophet's name in Avestan is Zarathustra (Persian Zardusht; Greek transcription Zoroaster). His date has never been settled. Tradition inside the community says the 6th century BCE, but mainstream scholarship pushes him much further back on linguistic grounds: the Old Avestan of the Gathas is a close cousin of the Sanskrit of the Rigveda and slightly later than it, which places the material somewhere between roughly 1500 and 900 BCE, with a figure around 1000 BCE most often adopted.

He did not build from nothing. He reformed an older Indo-Iranian sacrificial religion, elevating Ahura Mazda ("Lord of Wisdom") to sole creator and shifting the point of worship away from trading offerings for favors and toward moral choice. The scripture, the Avesta, was carried for well over a millennium by priestly recitation and written down very late; its oldest core is the seventeen hymns of the Gathas.

Under the Achaemenid Persians (550–330 BCE) Darius invoked Ahura Mazda repeatedly in his inscriptions, though whether the royal religion of that period already counts as "Zoroastrianism" is still argued. The tradition became fully institutional under the Sasanians (224–651 CE), who made it the imperial religion: the priesthood (the magi, today mobeds) was organized into a hierarchy tied closely to the throne — with the corresponding record of pressure on other faiths and on internal dissent.

In 651 the Sasanian state fell to the Arab conquest. What followed was not overnight conversion but attrition over centuries: the poll tax, legal standing, and the everyday pressures of marriage and advancement drained the community until it had contracted to the desert towns of Yazd and Kerman. Sometime between the 8th and 10th centuries a group crossed the Arabian Sea to Gujarat in India — the traditional account is the Qissa-i Sanjan, while archaeological evidence favors the earlier-to-mid 8th century — and became today's Parsis (literally "Persians"). In colonial Bombay in the 19th century, the Parsi community rose rapidly in trade, shipbuilding, education and philanthropy.

Distribution Today

Influence and numbers, wildly out of proportion

The global total is roughly 110,000–200,000: a 2012 study arrived at 112,000–122,000, while looser estimates run toward 200,000. The range is that wide because there is no agreed answer to who counts — ethnic descent, the children of mixed marriages, whether a person has been formally initiated. Change the rule and you change the number.

  • India: the largest community. Parsis in the Indian census fell from about 114,000 in 1941 to roughly 70,000 in 2001 and 57,000 in 2011; community bodies usually cite higher figures. Concentrated in Mumbai and Gujarat.
  • Iran: about 25,000 in the 2011 census, in Tehran, Yazd and Kerman. Zoroastrians are one of the religious minorities recognized in Iran's constitution, with a reserved seat in parliament.
  • Diaspora: roughly twenty thousand in North America (about 14,000 in the United States), with several thousand each in Britain, Canada, Australia and the Gulf states; Pakistan's 2023 census recorded around 2,300.

Structurally this is an ethnoreligion, not a universal missionary faith. Indian Parsi practice has traditionally not accepted outside converts — a community lawsuit decided in 1908 has long been cited as the basis — while Iran and parts of the diaspora hold looser positions. Add late marriage, low fertility, emigration and intermarriage, and the population curve has pointed downward for decades; since 2013 the Indian government has even run a Jiyo Parsi scheme funding fertility treatment and counselling. This is one of the very few religious traditions for which demography itself is the first-order issue.

Core Beliefs

A moral war inside a good world

One God and seven holy ones

Ahura Mazda is the sole creator: formless, supremely wise. Around him stand the Amesha Spentas, the "Bounteous Immortals" — good purpose, truth, right rule, devotion, wholeness, immortality. They are divine persons and at the same time virtues a human being can cultivate, which is the point worth pausing on: the attributes of God double as the curriculum for people. Beneath them is a further class of beings worthy of veneration (yazata), along with the fravashi, ancestral and guardian spirits.

Dualism, but not symmetry

The opposition between Ahura Mazda and the destructive spirit Angra Mainyu (Ahriman in Middle Persian) runs through the whole tradition — but what the Gathas emphasize is a primordial choice made by two mainyu, two mentalities. Evil is not a co-equal second creator; it is a destructive will, and its defeat is already determined. The more symmetrical version comes later, with Zurvanism. At the cosmic level the governing pair of terms is asha — truth, order, the way things ought to be — against druj, falsehood and distortion.

The material world is good

Here it parts company with many traditions. The created world is not illusion, prison or fallen ground; it is a good work, contaminated by evil, waiting to be repaired. The human task is therefore not to escape the world but to take the right side within it and help clean it up.

Eschatology: from personal judgment to world renewal

After death the soul crosses the Chinvat bridge, which narrows or widens according to a life's conduct — judgment at the individual scale. History's endpoint is the coming of a Saoshyant (savior), the resurrection of the dead, and the refining of all things in molten fire until evil is wholly removed. This final act is Frashokereti, literally "making wonderful" — the renewal of the world. Notably, its logic tends toward the universal: punishment is a purifying process rather than a permanent end state. The textual body runs from the Avesta (including the Gathas and the Yasna) to the Middle Persian commentaries such as the Denkard and the Bundahishn.

Practice & Principles

Good thoughts, good words, good deeds

The whole ethic compresses into three words: humata, hūkhta, huvarshta. It is emphatically not an ascetic path. Marrying and raising children, farming and irrigating, trading honestly, giving and relieving need are all counted as religious obligations, while renouncing the world to beg draws criticism. The Parsi community's unusually strong record in philanthropy and school-building follows directly from that premise.

  • Initiation (navjote, called sudreh-pushi in Iran): usually between ages seven and fifteen, when the child puts on the white cotton undershirt sudreh and is girded with the woolen cord kusti, woven from 72 threads for the 72 chapters of the Yasna. From then on the cord is untied and retied at each of the day's prayers.
  • Five watches (gah): dawn to noon, noon to mid-afternoon, mid-afternoon to nightfall, nightfall to midnight, midnight to dawn. One prays facing daylight, or a lamp or flame after dark.
  • Fire and temple: fire is the emblem of asha and the direction of prayer, not the object of worship. Temple fires are graded, and the highest, the "victorious fire" or Atash Behram, exists in only nine places on earth — eight in India, one in Yazd, Iran.
  • Purity and funerals: so that a corpse does not pollute earth, water or fire, the traditional practice is exposure in a dakhma, a "tower of silence." Since the 1990s India's vultures have all but vanished, poisoned by veterinary diclofenac; Mumbai now uses solar concentrators to aid decomposition, while some families have turned to cremation or burial — a dispute the community has yet to settle.
  • Festivals: Nowruz, the new year at the spring equinox (long since a cross-communal cultural holiday rather than a strictly religious one); the six seasonal Gahambars; Muktad, for remembering the departed; and autumn's Mehregan.

Internal differences

Indian Parsis and the later-arriving Iranis from Iran are two strands. Three calendars are in use — Shahenshahi, Kadmi and Fasli — so the same festival can fall a month apart. The priesthood (mobed) is traditionally hereditary. The sharpest disagreements concern conversion, intermarriage, women's participation and funeral practice: traditionalists defend the community's boundary, reformists argue for opening it, and both sides can cite text.

Common Misreadings

Misreading: they worship fire — hence "fire-worshippers."
Fire is the symbol of truth and the direction of prayer; the object of worship is always Ahura Mazda. Adherents also pray facing sunlight and clean water. "Fire-worship" is a label applied from outside, never a self-description.

Misreading: dualism means two equal gods at war.
The Gathas know one creator; evil is a destructive mentality and a choice, and its defeat is settled in advance. The more symmetrical picture belongs to the later Zurvanite current and is not the tradition's single settled view.

Misreading: this is an extinct religion of antiquity.
The numbers are tiny, but it functions: working fire temples and hereditary priests in Iran and India, and diaspora communities in North America, Britain and Australia still building new centers and youth organizations.

Misreading: Nietzsche's Zarathustra and the Magi of the Nativity are teaching this religion.
"Magi" does denote a Persian-Median priestly class, but Matthew's narrative has no direct doctrinal counterpart in Zoroastrian teaching, and Nietzsche borrowed only the name — the book's arguments have nothing to do with the tradition.

Across Traditions · The Contemporary Situation

Upstream: a shared root with the Vedas. The common Indo-Iranian origin left its fingerprints in the vocabulary: Sanskrit deva, "god," becomes Avestan daeva, "demon," while the asura line yields ahura, the highest being of all — the same words with their values inverted. Soma and haoma, Mitra and Mithra pair off the same way. So the Indian traditions of Days 9–10 are this tradition's estranged siblings, not its strangers.

Downstream: an influence hard to pin down. Most scholars hold that Second Temple Judaism was influenced during and after Persian rule in its angelology and demonology, its doctrine of resurrection and its picture of a final judgment, and that these passed through Judaism into Christianity and Islam (worth rereading against Days 1–7). But the reverse case has also been argued, and "who influenced whom, and how deeply" resists settlement, because the dates at which the key texts on both sides took their final form are themselves uncertain. What is clear is that Manichaeism drew heavily on Iranian dualist material.

Present tensions. The demographic crisis has turned "who counts as one of us" from a theological question into a question of survival: accepting converts and the children of mixed marriages would replenish the numbers, but it unsettles the ethnic boundary and the standing of community trust property, a particularly sensitive matter in the Indian context. Diaspora bodies building institutions in North America and Australasia often diverge from the traditional authorities in Mumbai. Nowruz points to another route entirely: inscribed on UNESCO's intangible heritage lists, it shows that what a tradition leaves behind can far outlive the size of its congregation.

Going Deeper

How does a religion that does not proselytize answer the arithmetic of demography?
Without conversion, numbers depend on births alone — and urbanization, high educational attainment, late marriage and emigration push fertility below replacement almost as a matter of course. That is hard arithmetic. Opening to converts solves the quantity but may change what the community is: where identity means ethnicity, property and memory at once, loosening the boundary rewrites "who we are." The problem is not unique here. Any tradition that binds religious identity to ethnic identity meets it (compare Day 1, Judaism); the smaller the community, the more urgent the question.
Where does the premise "the world is good as made" push a religion's ethics?
If the material world is a good work rather than a trap, then escaping it is not the practice — improving it is. The premise yields the ethical shape fairly directly: approval of marriage and childbearing and of productive labor, honest trade and irrigation and helping others counted as religious acts, and a low estimate of asceticism and renunciation. Conversely, traditions that read the phenomenal world as illusion or prison more readily develop paths of withdrawal and self-denial. The same question — how should a person live — gets very different answers depending on what the world is taken to be. It is one of the most explanatory contrasts in comparative religion.
What would it take to prove a claim of the form "A influenced B" in religious history?
Resemblance alone is not enough: parallel development, a shared earlier source, even borrowing in the other direction can all produce it. A strong argument usually needs three things at once — a documented point of contact in time and place (Jewish communities under Persian rule, for instance), evidence that the idea appears in the receiving texts genuinely later than that contact, and correspondence in structural detail rather than in broad theme. The difficulty is that the dates at which texts on both sides took final form often float by decades or centuries, and once they float, the direction of causation can flip. So "most scholars see influence, but its extent and route remain uncertain" is usually not evasion. It is the most honest statement the evidence supports.

This page introduces Zoroastrianism from a neutral, comparative standpoint, aiming to present its internal logic and diversity without praise, blame or ranking. Population figures are recent estimates and vary with how identity is defined (ethnic descent vs religious practice); scholarly disputes over dating and lines of influence are flagged in the text rather than resolved.
