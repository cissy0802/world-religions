World Religions

Shinto

Day 15East Asian traditionsJapan · ~80 million (see the counting problem below)

Around 60% of Japanese describe themselves as having no religion — and the country still logs well over 200 million shrine visits a year. That is not a contradiction so much as a clue: Shinto is not a set of propositions you are asked to believe, but a set of things you are expected to do. It has no founder, no creed, no original sin and no salvation. Even the name "Shinto" was coined only after Buddhism arrived, so that the local practice could be distinguished from the newcomer.

"Whatever is out of the ordinary — bird, beast, tree, grass, sea or mountain — and possesses superior, awe-inspiring power, is called kami."— Motoori Norinaga, Kojiki-den (18th century), still the most-quoted working definition of kami

History & Origins

A tradition named in hindsight

Shinto has no founding moment. Beneath it lie the archipelago's ancient rites for nature and ancestral spirits: each locality and clan venerated its own ujigami (tutelary kami), praying for harvest, safety and protection from plague, with no shared system and no shared theology.

The turning point came from outside. Buddhism arrived via the Korean peninsula in the 6th century, and for the first time the indigenous rites needed a name to stand against it — hence "Shinto," the way of the kami. In the 8th century the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) gathered scattered myths into a single genealogy and connected the imperial house to the sun kami Amaterasu; the 10th-century Engishiki then registered the state-supported shrines and their liturgies, and ritual became institutional.

For roughly the next thousand years the normal condition of Japanese religion was not "Shinto versus Buddhism" but shinbutsu shūgō, their amalgamation: temples stood inside shrine precincts and monks chanted sutras for the kami. The theory behind it was honji suijaku — kami were read as local traces of buddhas and bodhisattvas (Amaterasu, for instance, as a manifestation of Dainichi Nyorai). For most people this was one nested system, requiring no choice of sides.

After the Meiji Restoration the new government issued the shinbutsu bunri edicts of 1868, prising apart by decree what a millennium had entwined, and touching off a wave of anti-Buddhist destruction. The order that followed is usually called State Shinto: shrines were officially classed as "non-religious" state rites, folded into the machinery of government, and linked to the imperial institution and to schooling — scholars still debate whether this was a religion or a state ceremonial system. In 1945 the occupation authorities issued the Shinto Directive, ending state sponsorship and administration of shrines; the 1947 constitution established the separation of religion and state. The Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honchō), founded in 1946, reorganised most shrines as private religious corporations, returning Shinto to society itself.

Distribution Today

Eighty million, or three percent?

Shinto's numbers are the hardest in this series to read, because two methods of counting differ by more than twentyfold:

  • Institutional count: the Agency for Cultural Affairs' religious yearbook registers roughly 84–87 million Shinto adherents. But Japan's religious bodies together claim more adherents than the country has people (about 170 million against a population near 125 million). Shrines typically count the residents of their ujiko parish, and the same person is routinely counted by a shrine and a temple alike.
  • Self-report: in NHK's 2018 survey only about 3% identified as Shinto, against roughly 31% Buddhist and about 62% claiming no religion.

The more reliable measure is behaviour. Japan has roughly 80,000 shrines (about 78,000–79,000 of them within the Jinja Honchō system) and some 85,000 priests; an Agency for Cultural Affairs survey in 2021 estimated about 213 million shrine visits a year, roughly 60% of them clustered in the New Year hatsumōde. In other words, Shinto is measured by who shows up, not by who professes.

The trend runs two ways. One is contraction: rural depopulation is stranding small shrines, and the national total fell from about 86,600 in 2010 to roughly 80,000 by 2022 — some 6,000 closed or left untended in little over a decade, with one priest now commonly serving several shrines. The other is stability: hatsumōde, shichi-go-san and amulets have never really gone away.

Shinto does essentially no proselytising. It is a territorial and ethnic tradition rather than a universal missionary one; shrines abroad exist mainly where Japanese migrants settled — Hawaii, Brazil, North America — and remain few.

Core Belief System

Not what you believe, but what you live alongside

The myriad kami

Yaoyorozu — "eight million" — is rhetoric, not a census; it means uncountably many. A kami is not an omnipotent creator. It may be a mountain, a river, a rock or a storm; an outstanding ancestor or clan forebear; or the dreaded force behind an epidemic. The same kami holds both an aramitama (violent aspect) and a nigimitama (gentle aspect) — blessing and calamity issue from one source. Ritual therefore aims at pacification and coexistence, not at recruiting good gods against evil ones.

Pollution and purification: a system without original sin

Shinto's central axis is not good versus evil but purity (kiyome) versus pollution (kegare). Death, blood and illness bring kegare — which is not moral guilt but a condition that can be washed off, through harae (purification rites) and misogi (ablution in water). There is no final judgment, no doctrine of salvation, and no premise that human nature is fallen.

This-worldly focus, and the absence of a canon

Petitions are strikingly concrete: harvest, safe childbirth, warding off misfortune, good business, passing exams. Views of the afterlife stay vague, and historically the dead were entrusted to Buddhism. The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki are myth and dynastic history, not books of commandments; Shinto has no scriptural canon on the biblical model, no sayings of a founder, and no single body empowered to define orthodoxy — a structural contrast with the Abrahamic traditions.

Practice & Principles

Gesture before doctrine

Shinto is entered through the body: passing under a gate, rinsing hands and mouth, clapping. A visit follows a standard sequence.

The ritual year and the life cycle

  • Annual observances: New Year hatsumōde (by far the densest moment of the year), setsubun demon-expelling in February, the great purifications (ōharae) at midsummer and year's end that clear six months of accumulated pollution for the whole community, and each shrine's own reitaisai — the festival where the portable shrine is carried through the parish.
  • Life-cycle rites: the newborn's first shrine visit (hatsumiyamairi), shichi-go-san at ages three, five and seven, coming-of-age day, and Shinto weddings. Notably, the Shinto wedding is not ancient — it spread only in the early 20th century.
  • "Born Shinto, buried Buddhist": birth and growth belong to the shrine, funerals and memorial days almost always to the Buddhist temple. This division of labour is the practical inheritance of shinbutsu shūgō.
  • Wishes made material: omamori amulets, ema votive tablets, omikuji fortune slips; talismans are brought home to the household kamidana shelf, which coexists with the Buddhist altar under the same roof.

Renewal as permanence

At Ise Jingū the sanctuaries and sacred treasures are rebuilt from scratch every twenty years — the shikinen sengu, a practice traced to 690. The 62nd rebuilding took place in 2013; preparations for the 63rd began in 2025, with completion expected in 2033. It is at once a rite and a transmission mechanism for craft: every generation of carpenters must have built it once with their own hands.

Internal variation

Over three-quarters of shrines belong to Jinja Honchō, but major ones — Fushimi Inari Taisha and Yasukuni Shrine among them — stand outside it. Separate again is Sect Shinto: the thirteen groups classified in the Meiji era, such as Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō, which have founders, doctrines and congregations, and so resemble religious organisations in the ordinary sense far more than parish-based shrine Shinto does.

Common Misreadings

Misreading: Shinto is Japan's state religion.
The 1947 constitution mandates separation of religion and state; shrines are private religious corporations and cannot be publicly funded. The State Shinto system was dismantled in 1945.

Misreading: many gods means "primitive" — Shinto is an early stage of religion.
The evolutionary ladder model of religion was abandoned by scholars long ago. Shinto has elaborate ritual law, written mythology and its own theological traditions (Ise, Yoshida, and Restoration Shinto among them). It is not an unfinished monotheism.

Misreading: Japanese people say they have no religion, so shrines are just tourist sites.
In Japanese, mushūkyō usually means "not affiliated with a religious organisation," which sits comfortably alongside seasonal observance, buying amulets and holding shichi-go-san. Practice-centred religiosity was never going to show up in a survey about belief.

Misreading: Shinto and Buddhism are rivals.
They coexisted for close to a millennium and were separated by decree only in 1868. Most Japanese today use both systems and feel no need to choose.

Reading Across Traditions · The Contemporary Setting

The East Asian ritual sphere. Shinto shares with Confucianism (Day 14) and Daoism (Day 13) a regional pattern in which rite outranks creed and practice outranks profession, along with ancestral veneration and concerns about purity. But Shinto never developed a systematic ethics — historically Confucianism and Buddhism filled that space — and it has nothing like the Daoist pursuit of immortality or its ordination lineages.

Layering rather than exclusivity. Shinbutsu shūgō offers a model that stands in sharp contrast to the Abrahamic traditions (Days 1–8): religious identity can stack, divided by occasion, without mutual exclusion. It is also the key to Day 16 on East Asian folk religion.

Comparable to animism — up to a point. Kami are often read next to indigenous animist traditions (Day 19), and the intuition that the world is full of presences genuinely does connect them. But Shinto has written mythology, a national organisation and a history of state ceremonial, so filing it under "primitive animism" misdescribes it.

Live tensions. Yasukuni Shrine is the most watched of them. The shrine and some bereaved-family associations regard the enshrinement of the war dead as consolation of the dead within the scope of religious freedom; China, South Korea and other neighbours, along with a portion of opinion inside Japan, hold that because Class-A war criminals are among those enshrined, visits carry a judgment about history. Japanese courts have repeatedly heard suits over whether official visits breach the separation of religion and state, and have not converged on a single test. The question touches religious liberty, historical memory and diplomacy at once; this page sets out the positions without adjudicating between them. At the other end sits a far quieter problem: in depopulating regions shrines are losing their keepers and festivals are lapsing — arguably the more widespread challenge Shinto faces now.

Going Deeper

With no founder, no creed and no salvation, on what grounds is Shinto a "religion" at all?
It is worth noticing that the Japanese word for religion, shūkyō, was coined in the 19th century to translate "religion," on a template drawn from Christianity: creed, congregation, conversion, salvation. Measured with that ruler, Shinto fails on nearly every count. Switch to another set of indicators — communal observance through the year, embodied ritual, a strict grammar of sacred and profane space, coverage of every life transition — and its density is very high. Shinto is thus a methodological reminder: definition precedes measurement, and the ruler decides what you will see.
If pollution is not sin, how does such a system handle evil?
Purification treats the negative as something adhering to a person rather than as corruption of their nature. The advantage is that guilt does not accumulate: individuals and communities can periodically reset. The cost is the absence of an internal mechanism of moral accountability — Shinto itself offers no strong account of responsibility for wrongdoing, and historically Confucian norms and Buddhist karma supplied it. Set this beside the Jewish notion of two inclinations and the Christian doctrine of original sin: each tradition's answer to "why do people do harm?" largely determines what institutions it then has to build.
If Ise is torn down and rebuilt every twenty years, where is the original?
This directly challenges an idea of authenticity grounded in material continuity: if no timber in the sanctuary is twenty years old, is it still that shrine? Shinto's answer locates continuity in form, technique and the handing on of skill rather than in the wood — what is transmitted is the making, not the object. The argument has since entered international heritage debates, pressing the recognition that for wooden and living traditions authenticity can be guaranteed by transmission rather than by original material. The question can also be turned around: if continuity rests on transmission, what remains of a rebuilt structure once transmission has broken?
