Daoism 道教

Day 13East Asian Traditions~9–20 million self-identified

Plenty of people have read the Daodejing; far fewer have seen Daoism. The first is a philosophical classic translated into dozens of languages. The second is a living religion with a pantheon, a canon, an ordained clergy and a liturgy — priests who perform rites for a neighbourhood, adepts who treat their own bodies as the crucible for refining "inner alchemy." The two are usually taught apart. They are really the upper and lower reaches of one river.

"Humans follow earth, earth follows heaven, heaven follows the Dao, and the Dao follows its own nature."— Daodejing, chapter 25

History & Origins

From a body of thought to an organised religion

Daoism has two headwaters. One is pre-imperial Daoist thought: the Daodejing (also called the Laozi) and the Zhuangzi. Bamboo slips excavated at Guodian, Hubei in 1993 — from a tomb dated to roughly 300 BCE — already contain passages of the Laozi, but with different chapter order and wording, amounting to about two-fifths of the received text. Most scholars therefore read it as a text that accumulated over time through many hands rather than the work of one author in one sitting; the biographical record for Laozi himself is extremely thin. The second headwater is older and less often mentioned: the arts of the occult, spirit-mediums, immortality-seeking and longevity practice.

What braided the two into an organised religion is conventionally dated to 142 CE, when Zhang Daoling, in what is now Sichuan, announced that the deified Laozi had conferred on him the "Covenant with the Powers of Orthodox Unity." His Way of the Celestial Masters — popularly the "Five Pecks of Rice," after the annual contribution members made — had precepts, parishes and an ordained hierarchy, and for a time governed a semi-theocratic polity in the Hanzhong region. A parallel movement, the Way of Great Peace, was tied to the Yellow Turban uprising of 184. From the very beginning, Daoism was both a path of cultivation and a form of social organisation.

The fourth to sixth centuries built the scriptural architecture: the Shangqing and Lingbao revelations gave Daoism a systematic canon, pantheon and ritual code, and under the Northern Wei, Kou Qianzhi won state patronage for the Celestial Masters. Tang emperors honoured Laozi as an imperial ancestor, and "outer alchemy" — compounding mineral elixirs for longevity — flourished, until lead and mercury poisoning shifted the search inward.

The pivotal turn came around 1170, when Wang Chongyang founded the Quanzhen ("Complete Perfection") school in Shandong: monastic celibacy, an openness to Confucian and Buddhist thought, and above all inner alchemy, which treats the body as the furnace in which essence is refined into breath and breath into spirit. His disciple Qiu Chuji was summoned by the Mongol court in 1222, and Quanzhen rose to dominance. From the Ming onward the landscape settled into two great orders: Zhengyi (householder priests, masters of talismans and liturgy, seated at Mount Longhu in Jiangxi) and Quanzhen (monastic, as at the White Cloud Temple in Beijing). The imperially sponsored Zhengtong Daozang was printed in 1445 — over five thousand fascicles containing roughly 1,400–1,500 texts, still the base library for the tradition.

The modern era brought rupture. Classed as "superstition" under the impact of Western learning, then battered by war and by twentieth-century political campaigns, temples closed and transmission lines broke. Rebuilding began in the 1980s and the gaps are still being filled.

Where It Stands Today

One of the hardest religions to count

The counting problem is itself instructive: East Asian religious practice generally does not run on exclusive membership. Someone may draw divination lots at a temple, hire a Daoist priest for a funeral, sweep the ancestral graves at Qingming and burn incense on a deity's birthday, and still never say "I am a Daoist." Change the definition and the figure changes by an order of magnitude:

  • Narrow (self-identification): the World Religion Database gives roughly 8.7 million; a commonly cited figure is about 20 million.
  • Broad (counting temple-based popular religion as Daoist practice): well over 100 million, with published estimates ranging from tens of millions to some 170 million. The spread reflects definitions, not disputed facts.

Geographically the tradition is concentrated in the Chinese-speaking world:

  • Mainland China: several thousand registered Daoist venues. Clergy counts vary with the criteria — official figures commonly cite about 40,000, while estimates from the China Daoist Association, which include the many Zhengyi priests living dispersed among the population, run to 50,000–100,000. Major seats and sacred mountains include Longhu, Wudang, Qingcheng and Qiyun, and Beijing's White Cloud Temple.
  • Taiwan: an Academia Sinica survey in 2021 found about 18.7% identifying as Daoist, and Daoist temples make up the largest share of registered religious buildings — close to ten thousand — though "Daoist" in the registry covers popular temples broadly.
  • Singapore: 8.8% in the 2020 census, some 300,000 people; stable communities also exist in Hong Kong, Malaysia, Vietnam and Chinese diaspora populations worldwide.

Daoism is not a universalising missionary religion but a cultural-regional one: it travels with Chinese migration and rarely recruits across cultural lines. Meanwhile taiji, qigong, fengshui and Daoist longevity regimens attract wide global interest — but that is largely borrowed practice, which is a different thing from conversion.

Core Beliefs

One breath through all things; long life and union with the Dao

The Dao is not a god

The Dao is not a willing creator but the source and the way of the working of all things — "the Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao." That single point marks the deepest divergence from the Abrahamic traditions: there is no legislating creator and no absolute rupture between god and world. Wuwei, correspondingly, is not passivity but the refusal to force — not acting against the grain of a situation.

Qi, yin-yang, five phases

The cosmos consists of qi (vital breath), which differentiates into yin and yang and circulates through the five phases; the human body is structurally identical to the cosmos — a microcosm. That premise yields the logic of Daoist cultivation directly: if body and universe share one architecture, then ordering your own qi is participation in cosmic order. Chinese medicine, fengshui and inner alchemy all rest on this same cosmology.

A pantheon shaped like a bureaucracy

Daoism has a vast pantheon, headed by the Three Pure Ones (the Celestial Worthies of Primordial Beginning, of Numinous Treasure, and of the Way and its Virtue — the last being the deified Laozi), with the Jade Emperor and countless astral, local and occupational deities below. Crucially, the gods are inside the cosmos of qi: they hold ranks and offices and can rise or fall. Heaven is imagined as an administration, and priests submit written memorials to it much as officials submit documents to a court — an unmistakably Chinese way of picturing the divine.

Not the far shore, but union with the Dao

The goal is neither heaven after death nor escape from rebirth, but long life and union with the Dao — becoming an "immortal" (xian). That word carries several readings, from bodily ascension and transformation through apparent death to a more inward merging of spirit with the Dao. Beyond the Daodejing and Zhuangzi, key scriptures include the Taiping jing, the Huangting jing, the Zhouyi cantong qi and the Baopuzi, all gathered in the Daoist canon.

Principles & Practice

Ritual turned outward, alchemy turned inward

Daoist practice runs in two directions, usually carried by different people.

  • Outward: liturgy and communal rites. Priests conduct services for a community — blessing, exorcism, releasing the dead, and large offering festivals — using talismans (writings that carry divine force) and memorials addressed to the celestial administration. For most laypeople this is what Daoism primarily is: services one can call on in a concrete difficulty — a child, an illness, a funeral, an auspicious date.
  • Inward: inner alchemy and longevity practice. Sitting meditation, circulating breath, guided stretching, holding stillness — the "dual cultivation of nature and life," where life means bodily energy and nature means the heart-mind. This path requires long transmission from a master and forms the daily discipline of monastic adepts.

Ethics: ledgers of merit, and inherited consequence

Alongside the three refuges and five precepts, Daoism developed the distinctive "ledger of merit and demerit" — a daily accounting of good and bad acts. A second concept is chengfu: the consequences of one's conduct extend to descendants and others, which computes differently from Buddhist karma settling on an individual across rebirths. Morality tracts such as the Taishang ganying pian circulated very widely.

Festivals and everyday life

The three Yuan festivals, the birthdays of individual deities, rites for the year-god Taisui, lamp-lighting for peace, temple fairs and pilgrimage. Unlike the Abrahamic traditions, Daoism generally does not legislate the whole of a follower's life: most participation is on an as-needed basis — not a deficit of devotion, but a structural feature of the East Asian religious ecology.

How the two orders differ

  • Zhengyi: mostly householders, free to marry, often with a secular occupation; specialists in talismans and liturgy, transmitting through master-disciple or family lines, deeply embedded in local communities.
  • Quanzhen: monastic and celibate, vegetarian, centred on inner alchemy and the rule of the temple.

Common Misreadings

Misreading: Daoism is just the philosophy in the Daodejing.
The philosophical texts and the organised religion are two stretches of one river. Temple Daoism has a pantheon, a canon, ordained clergy and liturgy; the impression that "Daoism is philosophy, not religion" owes much to selective modern translation and to an assumption that philosophy ranks above religion.

Misreading: wuwei means doing nothing.
It means not forcing, not working against the grain. Much of the Daodejing is in fact about governing — how to disturb least and still get things done, closer to a method for acting than a licence for idleness.

Misreading: fortune-telling, fengshui and qigong are Daoism.
They overlap with it, but each also has independent roots, and much of their circulation has been popular rather than clerical. Equating them with Daoism is like summarising a religion by its peripheral practices.

Misreading: Daoists are white-bearded hermits on mountains.
Most Zhengyi priests have families and day jobs, performing rites for neighbours in the evenings and at festivals; Quanzhen clergy are celibate but many live in urban temples. Reclusion exists — it is simply far from the norm.

Comparative Reading · Contemporary Situation

Confucian, Daoist, Buddhist — not a choice of one. The norm in East Asian religious history is that a single person served in office as a Confucian, cultivated health as a Daoist, and buried the dead as a Buddhist. The "unity of the three teachings" is not vagueness but a division of labour (Days 14 and 16 take this up). Daoism and Buddhism competed for centuries and reshaped each other in the process: Daoism absorbed rebirth, hells and monastic organisation, while Buddhism took on local deities and indigenous ritual forms.

Resemblance to Indian traditions, and real difference. Inner alchemy works with circulating breath and the transformation of essence, breath and spirit; yoga works with breath, energy centres and upward movement. Both treat the body as the site of practice, and the technical parallels are genuine. The aims diverge: long life and union with the Dao versus liberation from rebirth. Close resemblances are the most rewarding comparisons and the easiest to flatten.

An echo in mysticism. "The Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao" runs structurally parallel to the apophatic path in Jewish, Christian and Islamic mysticism — that whatever can be said is not God. Day 22 gathers that thread.

Present tensions. The tourist economy and commercialisation of temples; the rebuilding of lineages broken in the twentieth century, and the question of what counts as authentic transmission; the de-religionised borrowing of longevity practice, taiji and mindfulness as they globalise; and the frequent invocation of Daoism as a resource for environmental ethics — a reading with real textual footing, though scholars caution against turning ancient texts directly into modern policy.

Going Deeper

Why is "how many Daoists are there" almost unanswerable?
Because the question smuggles in assumptions: that a person belongs to exactly one religion and can report it. That framework comes from the census experience of Abrahamic societies and misfires in East Asia, where religion behaves less like exclusive membership and more like a repertoire of services and seasonal rhythms one draws on as needed. So the narrow figures (a few million to twenty million) and the broad ones (well over a hundred million) are both defensible — they answer different questions. The lesson generalises: with cross-cultural religious statistics, always ask how the counting was done.
What two kinds of religion follow from treating the body as a workshop versus as an obstacle?
Daoist inner alchemy makes the body the furnace: longevity and union with the Dao are accomplished through this body, which is why the tradition generated vast technical systems of medicine, movement, diet and breath, and why religion and health practice are hard to separate within it. Where the body is framed instead as a fetter or a trial, cultivation tends toward asceticism, restraint and transcendence of the flesh. Compare Jain austerity (Day 11), aimed at exhausting karma and leaving the body behind. The same phrase — cultivating the self — points in opposite directions under different metaphysics.
What does imagining heaven as a bureaucracy reveal about religion and social structure?
The Daoist celestial realm has ranks, offices and paperwork, and priests submit memorials as officials submit documents — very nearly a mirror of the imperial bureaucracy. Similar mappings are everywhere: "God is king" in monotheism, the mutual warrant of social and cosmic order (dharma) in Indian traditions. Religious imagination tends to organise the transcendent using whatever social experience is at hand. The more interesting question runs the other way: once that social experience disappears, how does the religious imagination rewrite itself?

This page introduces Daoism from a neutral, comparative standpoint, aiming to present the tradition's internal logic and internal diversity without endorsement, criticism or ranking. Population figures are recent estimates and carry wide ranges because "religious identity" is defined differently across sources.
