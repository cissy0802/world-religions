World Religions

Mysticism, Shared and Not

Day 22ComparativeA minority path inside every tradition

Mysticism is often taken as a synonym for the vague and the occult. Inside the traditions themselves it is something else: the most demanding technical path they offer — a long training of attention, breath, appetite and daily schedule, aimed at reaching, in this life, somewhere ordinary language cannot go. What makes it worth studying comparatively is a double fact. Practitioners from traditions that never met describe the journey in startlingly similar terms; and they disagree, irreconcilably, about what it is they arrived at. That combination of agreement and disagreement is one of the better keys to religion as a whole.

"That which is the subtle essence — this whole world has it as its self… You are that."— Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7 (Tat tvam asi), the most quoted line in the Indian traditions

History & Origins

Four paths that never met, arriving at a similar shape

Start with the word. "Mysticism" descends from the Greek mystikos, tied to the ancient mystery rites and meaning roughly "closed-lipped, not to be passed on"; early Christians used it for the hidden sense of scripture and sacrament. Lifting it out as a cross-religious category is the work of European scholarship from the late 19th and early 20th centuries — like the theism/atheism grid of Day 21, it is an imported measuring tool. The traditions do not call themselves by it. They say "the path" (tariqa), "what has been received" (kabbalah), "yoking, union" (yoga), "stillness" (hesychia). An outside ruler buys you comparison; the price is that unlike things get flattened into one.

India systematized earliest. The Upanishads (c. 800–500 BCE) set out the identity of Brahman and atman; Patanjali's Yoga Sutras (dated across a wide range, from the 2nd century BCE to the 4th century CE) break the practice into eight limbs and offer a near-operational definition: yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind.

Judaism runs from Merkavah mysticism (c. 2nd–7th centuries CE, ascent meditations built on Ezekiel's chariot vision) to the Kabbalah of medieval Spain. The Zohar circulated from the 1280s through Moses de León, attributed to the 2nd-century Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai; modern scholarship largely credits de León himself. After the 1492 expulsion from Spain, Isaac Luria (1534–1572) in Safed, Galilee, built a cosmology that reread exile itself as cosmic breakage and repair.

Christianity begins with the 4th-century Egyptian desert fathers, their solitude and ceaseless prayer. The East developed this into hesychasm: the Jesus Prayer repeated with a fixed posture and breath. Gregory Palamas (1296–1359) defended it at several councils in Constantinople between 1341 and 1351, establishing the distinction between God's essence, forever unknowable, and God's energies, which can be experienced. The Philokalia, printed at Venice in 1782, put the line back into circulation. The Latin West produced Meister Eckhart — Pope John XXII's 1329 bull In agro dominico judged 17 of 28 propositions heretical and 11 ill-phrased — and, in 16th-century Spain, Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582) and John of the Cross (1542–1591), whose "dark night of the soul" reads as a map.

Islam was covered in Day 8: ascetics and the practice of remembrance from the 8th century, brought inside mainstream theology by al-Ghazali (1058–1111), taken to the "oneness of being" by Ibn Arabi (1165–1240) and to poetry by Rumi (1207–1273), and consolidated into the orders between the 12th and 15th centuries.

Where It Is Today

A path you cannot take a census of

Mysticism is not a religion with adherents to count; it is a route inside traditions. Three oblique measures are what we have.

One: the organized form is small. In Pew Research Center's 2012 survey of Muslims across 39 countries, the share identifying with a Sufi order was in the single digits in most countries; it passed ten percent only in Bangladesh (26%), Russia (19%), Tajikistan (18%), Pakistan (17%), Malaysia (17%), Albania (13%) and Uzbekistan (11%). The pattern generalizes: full-time practitioners are a minority everywhere, while the poetry, music and texts they produce travel far beyond their numbers.

Two: self-reported experience is common. In a 2009 Pew survey, 49% of Americans said they had had a "religious or mystical experience"; a 1962 Gallup poll using a comparable question found 22%. Pew's 2023 survey found 45% reporting "a sudden feeling of connection with something from beyond this world" and 30% reporting an encounter with an unseen spiritual force. Such figures are highly sensitive to wording and come almost entirely from US samples; treat them as orders of magnitude, not global rates.

Three: the techniques are decoupling from the traditions. The US National Health Interview Survey found in 2022 that 18.3% of adults had meditated in the past year (roughly 60 million people) and 16.9% had practiced yoga — both far above their 2002 levels. The methods have been lifted out, stripped of their original destination, and refitted into frameworks of health and performance. That is the largest change of our era, and it reaches further than any doctrinal argument.

The Belief System

Similar road, incompatible destinations

The recurring description of the path

Read the first-person accounts side by side and a common vocabulary appears. First purification (discipline, repentance, letting go of attachment), then concentration (the mind held on one point: a name, a short prayer, a breath), then reports of time and space giving way and the subject–object boundary dissolving, along with an unusually strong conviction that this is more real than ordinary life. Nearly every tradition then calls it ineffable, and nearly every one turns to negation: the Upanishads' "not this, not that" (neti neti), Pseudo-Dionysius's divine darkness, the Daodejing's "the way that can be spoken is not the constant way."

But the destinations exclude one another

In monotheist traditions, union must be phrased as joining with God, not becoming God — cross that line and you are a heretic, which is what the Eckhart propositions and the case of the Sufi al-Hallaj turn on. Advaita Vedanta says you already are Brahman: nothing is achieved, something is recognized. Buddhism says no-self and emptiness, denying even a self that could be united with anything (Buddhist thought is not covered on this site; Day 9 and Day 11 give the Indian background). One reported road, three mutually incompatible readings.

The scholarly split

Perennialism — W. T. Stace's Mysticism and Philosophy (1960) is the standard reference — holds that the core experience is the same and the doctrines are later packaging. Constructivism — Steven Katz's essays from 1978 onward — answers that there is no such thing as an unmediated experience: doctrine and method shape the experience before it occurs, so a Christian's union with God and a Buddhist's emptiness are not two accounts of one thing. The argument is unsettled, but both sides accept one floor: similar descriptions do not license the inference that the experiences are the same.

Principles & Practice

A craft with a teacher, a daily quota, and brakes

  • Ethics first. Almost every tradition puts moral purification before experience. The first two limbs of Patanjali's eight are restraints and observances; Sufism calls it tazkiyat al-nafs, the polishing of the self; the Carmelites treat the acquisition of virtue as a precondition of the dark night. Skipping this step is treated internally as danger, not shortcut.
  • Lineage and authorization. The shaykh, the guru, the Orthodox elder (geron), the Catholic spiritual director. The reasoning is consistent: this road amplifies self-deception, and telling the real from the counterfeit needs an outside eye. Which also means the structure carries an inherent risk of abuse — and criticism of abusive teachers is itself an old genre inside these traditions.
  • Bodily technique. Breath (pranayama; the Jesus Prayer coordinated with inhalation and exhalation), posture, repetition (dhikr, the Jesus Prayer, mantra, the rosary), fasting, vigils, retreat (the Sufi forty-day khalwa, the Christian Lent), and music and movement (whirling, qawwali, the Hasidic niggun).
  • Made into a daily rule. Not waiting on an experience but doing a fixed quantity every day for decades. This is the sharpest practical difference between contemplative practice and experience-seeking.
  • Brakes are standard equipment. Patanjali lists the attainments (siddhi) explicitly as obstacles; Orthodoxy has a diagnostic term, prelest, for mistaking delusion for grace; Sufism distinguishes the passing state (hal) from the settled station (maqam). All are worried about the same failure: an intense experience convinces you that you have arrived while nothing has actually changed. The internal test therefore lands on ordinary character, not on the intensity of the experience.
  • A cyclical relation to the institution. Suspected, then absorbed, then part of the orthodoxy. Hesychasm, Sufism and Kabbalah all traced that curve.

Common Misreadings

Misreading: mysticism is about visions, trances and powers.
Most traditions explicitly classify these as obstacles or by-products and coined warning terms for them (siddhi, prelest). The internal pass mark is usually a change in ordinary character — patience, compassion, honesty.

Misreading: all mysticisms are really saying the same thing, and religious difference is just the wrapping.
That is the perennialist position, not a scholarly consensus. Constructivists argue that doctrine shapes experience before it happens, and the traditions' verdicts on the destination exclude one another. Similar wording is not identical experience.

Misreading: mystics are natural anti-institutionalists.
The tension is real, but most mystics were observant insiders: al-Ghazali was a jurist, Teresa reformed her order from within the church, and hesychasm ended up as part of Orthodox orthodoxy. The suppressed cases are famous precisely because they are exceptions.

Misreading: meditation is a neutral, risk-free general-purpose tool.
The technique can certainly be used on its own, but in its home tradition it sits inside ethics, lineage and a specific goal; clinical research has also reported adverse effects in a minority of practitioners. Remove the frame and you have something else — not necessarily worse, but genuinely different.

Reading Across Traditions · The Present

It tends to push a tradition toward the far end of the spectrum. Picking up Day 21's map of the divine: the mystical route inside most traditions slides toward panentheism — the Sufi oneness of being, the Kabbalistic Ein Sof, the Orthodox doctrine of deification (theosis). That is why it keeps turning up at the edge of orthodoxy: read with orthodox grammar, what it says is always half a step off.

Interreligious dialogue often starts here. A good deal of the substantive cross-tradition exchange of the 20th century happened between contemplatives — Cistercian monks with Zen practitioners, with Hindu ascetics. The reason is not mysterious: what they share is method and difficulty rather than propositions, and propositions are the hardest part to agree on.

Two contemporary pressures. First, the rise of "spiritual but not religious" takes mystical experience out of the traditions and keeps the technique (Days 25 and 26 will take this up). Second, consciousness science has made it an experimental object: in Walter Pahnke's 1962 Good Friday experiment in Boston, ten theology students received psilocybin and eight met the criteria for a "complete" mystical experience; the 2006 double-blind study by Roland Griffiths's team at Johns Hopkins brought the line back into mainstream journals. Which surfaces an old question in a new form — if it can be chemically occasioned and neurally imaged, does that settle what it is? Answers diverge, and this is not a question more data can close.

Going Deeper

If a mystical experience can be chemically induced, is it still "real"?
There is a skipped step in the usual argument: from "it has a neurochemical correlate" to "it is only a neurochemical event." By the same reasoning, vision can be induced with an electrode, yet we do not conclude the table we see is fake — how something occurs and the standing of what it discloses are two questions. Running the other way, the traditions were already cautious here: they never treated the experience itself as evidence, only whether it produced a lasting change in character.
Why does nearly every tradition call it ineffable and then leave a mountain of writing?
"Ineffable" is usually not a refusal to speak but a label on how the words that follow should be used: these are signposts, do not mistake them for descriptions. Hence the heavy use of negation (not this, not that), paradox (a luminous darkness), poetry and analogy — devices that make language point past itself. There is a disciplinary motive too: a settled answer lets later practitioners set out carrying someone else's conclusion, and these traditions hold that the road has to be walked personally. Worth asking, though, whether "ineffable" also becomes a formula that shields a claim from ever being tested.
What is gained and what is lost when meditation is lifted out of its tradition?
Gained: accessibility and researchability. You can use it for stress, therapy or attention without signing up to a metaphysics, and only then can it enter a randomized controlled trial. Lost: at least three things — the ethical precondition, the lineage (someone whose job is to catch your self-deception), and the goal itself, since the original aim was total transformation of the self, not a better working week. That need not be a decline; it can be read as a translation. But translation always loses something, and what it loses here is the part the source tradition considered most important.
