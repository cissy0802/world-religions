Indigenous & Shamanic Traditions

Day 19Ancient & Minority Traditions≈476 million Indigenous people worldwide

Every one of the previous eighteen entries had a clear object: a founder, a scripture, a doctrine. This one has none. "Indigenous and shamanic traditions" is not a tradition; it is thousands of unrelated traditions bundled together by a foreign word. "Shaman" began as a single word in a single Siberian language, was carried back to Europe in the seventeenth century, and has been applied to the whole planet ever since. More counterintuitive still: most of these traditions had no word for "religion" at all.

A core scriptural line would normally go here.It can't. These traditions are carried almost entirely by oral transmission, and sacred speech is bound to particular people, places and occasions — copying it out of its occasion already changes it. This empty slot is the first thing to understand.

History & Origins

The oldest layer, and the hardest to write a history of

Strictly speaking there is no "origin" to recover — no founding year, no roster of first believers, only each people's own origin narratives and whatever archaeology can cautiously read. Just one thing is certain: long before the traditions that left written records, human religious life was already running in this form.

The part that has an archive is almost entirely a history recorded by outsiders and reshaped by outsiders. "Shaman" comes from šaman in Evenki, a Tungusic language of Siberia; the root is usually traced to "to know." It entered Western Europe in the late seventeenth century through travellers such as the Dutchman Nicolaes Witsen, and reached English in the merchant Adam Brand's 1698 travel account. A local job title thereby became a global classification.

The rupture

The three centuries that followed brought large-scale suppression and replacement, along strikingly similar paths:

  • Northern Europe: in the early 1700s the missionary Thomas von Westen and colleagues, regarding the Sámi drum as "the Sámi Bible," seized them systematically; over a hundred were shipped to Copenhagen and most were destroyed in a 1728 fire. Fewer than 80 original drums survive in museums worldwide.
  • North America: Canada amended the Indian Act in 1885 to ban the potlatch and the Sun Dance, a prohibition lifted only in 1951; the U.S. Department of the Interior's 2022–2024 investigation confirmed 417 federal Indian boarding schools and at least 973 children who died while attending; Canada's recognised residential schools number 139 (1831–1998).
  • Siberia: under Soviet rule shamans were treated as "superstition," ritual objects were confiscated, and practice moved out of sight.

The swing back

The turn came late in the twentieth century, mostly in law: the U.S. American Indian Religious Freedom Act (1978), the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (1990) and the 1994 amendment covering ceremonial peyote use; and the UN General Assembly's 2007 Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, whose Article 12 explicitly names the right to practise ceremonies, access sacred sites, control ceremonial objects and repatriate human remains.

Distribution Today

Three numbers that count three different populations

Nowhere else in this series is it more important not to add the figures together. Three common rulers do not nest inside one another:

  • Identity count: the figure used by the UN and the ILO is over 476 million Indigenous people in some 90 countries, about 6.2% of the world's population, belonging to more than 5,000 distinct groups. That counts ethnic identity, not belief.
  • Religious classification: Pew Research's 2020 estimate puts adherents of "folk and traditional religions" at roughly 429 million, about 6% of humanity. But that basket also holds Chinese folk religion and African traditional religions (Day 20), and overlaps only partly with "Indigenous."
  • Self-report: in Australia's 2021 census only 7,887 people wrote "Australian Aboriginal traditional religion" in the religion field — while nearly a million identified as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander in the same census. The gap is not disappearance: many are baptised and also carry obligations to Country, and the form allows only one answer.

Continuity is strongest across Amazonia, the Andes, North America, the Arctic, Siberia, inland Australia and the Pacific islands. Siberia offers a documented revival: in 2005 Tuva had 8 registered shamanic organisations and 57 certified shamans on the books, while researchers estimated the number actually practising in the hundreds.

Typologically this is neither an "ethnic religion" nor a "universalising missionary religion" but a place-bound one: a tradition is tied to a specific land and a specific set of kin, is in principle not transplantable, and has no concept of mission or conversion. That explains both why it rarely expands and why losing land amounts to losing the tradition.

Core Belief System

No shared doctrine, but recurring family resemblances

The world is inhabited by more than humans

The word usually translated "animism" invites the caricature of treating stones as alive. The more accurate statement is that the boundary of "person" is drawn wider than moderns draw it: bears, salmon, thunder, a particular outcrop are often called "other-than-human persons" in anthropology — they have intentions, they have face, they hold grudges and they repay. This is not lyricism but a source of concrete rules: ask permission before hunting, return the first portion, observe quotas, dispose of the bones properly, or the animal will not come back next year.

Reciprocity rather than worship

The governing verbs are usually not "believe" and "worship" but feed, thank, exchange, repair the relationship. Consequently these traditions rarely develop a believer/unbeliever divide and rarely police heresy: what counts as going wrong is normally doing it wrong — sequence, taboo, an unpaid debt — rather than thinking it wrong.

The land is the scripture

In central Australia, Tjukurpa is often rendered "the Dreamtime," but it does not denote a finished antiquity: the acts of ancestral beings laid down the shape of the land and the Law for people, and remain in force in the present tense. It is carried in songlines — long sung sequences naming landscape features in the order a traveller meets them, functioning at once as map, law and ecological archive. The scripture is not in a book; it is underfoot.

Mediators and a layered world

The cosmology typical of "shaman"-type roles is tiered: an upper world, the middle world of humans, a lower world. After training, the shaman enters an altered state with drum, song, fasting or (region-dependent) plants and travels on others' behalf — retrieving a strayed soul, negotiating, bringing back word. But this model comes from the Siberian–Inner Asian zone and does not generalise: many traditions whose specialists outsiders call "shamans" have no ecstatic journey at all, mediating instead through possession, divination, dreams or ritual knowledge.

Principles & Practice

Not chosen, but selected

Becoming a mediator is usually not voluntary: the standard routes are an illness that will not heal, a near-death experience, recurring dreams, or descent, and refusal is often held to make the illness worse. Years of apprenticeship follow: repertoire, taboos, plants, making the instruments, genealogy. This is a different logic of qualification from "attend seminary, receive ordination."

  • Rites: healing and soul retrieval, divination, permission for hunting and weather, naming, initiation, marriage and death, escorting the dead. Most are scheduled by season and animal cycles rather than a fixed calendar.
  • Objects: drums, masks, poles, medicine bundles. These are typically regarded as persons and partners rather than props, which is why confiscation was so destructive.
  • Ethics: reciprocity and restraint — take in measure, repay what is owed, return the first portion to its source. In context this is an obligation, not an environmental strategy.
  • Tiered knowledge: much of it is restricted by gender, age, family and initiation grade, so disclosure can itself be a violation — a "complete account" of these traditions is impossible in principle.

Who practises today: three routes

All three use the word "shaman," but the source of authority differs completely. Continuity in place rests on unbroken apprenticeship and a relationship to land. Institutional hybrids are typified by the Native American Church, formed on the Plains in the late nineteenth century, which combines the peyote sacrament with Christian vocabulary and a formal charter; membership is commonly estimated at about 250,000 (some sources say over 300,000), and the 1994 amendment gave its ceremonial use a clear legal footing. Urban neo-shamanism dates from the 1980 book The Way of the Shaman and the "core shamanism" courses that followed, which hold that a universal technique can be extracted from its cultural context — a claim widely contested within Indigenous communities as appropriation stripped of accountability.

Common Misreadings

Misreading: "shamanism" is a religion.
It is a classification, not a self-description — the word comes from Evenki and was applied by outside scholarship to ritual specialists everywhere. Comparative works such as the 1951 French study Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy treated it as a universal type, and have drawn sustained anthropological criticism ever since: the label erases sharply different sources of authority and function.

Misreading: these are living fossils, frozen in place.
They have always changed, and today's forms are typically products of long interaction with colonisation, mission and markets: the Native American Church is itself a late-nineteenth-century institution, and Siberia's registered shamanic organisations are a creation of the 1990s.

Misreading: psychoactive plants are standard equipment.
It varies by region. Amazonia does centre plants such as ayahuasca; the Siberian mainstay is drum and song; Australian traditions largely do not involve them. Treating one region's practice as the global rule is precisely the illusion this label keeps producing.

Misreading: Indigenous traditional religion has essentially died out.
Low census figures usually reflect incompatibility with a "pick one religion" format rather than disappearance. Large numbers of practitioners simultaneously hold Christian or Muslim identities while ritual life continues at the level of family and land — it simply does not surface in the statistics.

Cross-Tradition Reading · Contemporary Situation

Sideways: East Asia. The kami of Day 15 and the local deities of Day 16 likewise have no founder, no unified scripture and more ritual than creed, and likewise bind the sacred to particular places; the Daoist rites of Day 13 — summoning deities, expelling malign influence — are functional neighbours, and along Northeast China and the Mongolian plateau the two zones interpenetrated for centuries. The divergence is that all the East Asian strands became institutionalised — a national shrine system, ordination registers and orders, written liturgy — so a specialist's legitimacy derives from lineage rather than a personal call.

Contemporary: a sacred site is not a building. Today's sharpest conflicts are almost all about land. A mountain, a river, a burial ground can usually only be classified by modern law as property or as an environmental resource, and is hard to recognise as a place of worship — which is why pipeline, mining and dam disputes are seldom litigated in religious-freedom terms. The contrast is the legalisation of ritual substances: the 1994 peyote provision, and the U.S. Supreme Court's unanimous 2006 ruling on a Brazilian tradition's ayahuasca tea, both show that once a practice is translated into the language of "religion," protection becomes far easier to obtain — and that translation is exactly what many traditions are least willing to make.

Going Deeper

Can the frame of "religion" hold them at all?
"Religion" as a domain separable from economy, law and kinship is a concept formed in early modern Europe and then pushed worldwide with colonisation and statistical administration. Used to carve up a way of life that binds hunting quotas, kin obligations and ritual together, it tends to produce something unrecognisable. But refusing the frame has costs too: legal protection, sacred-site recognition and repatriation of regalia must all be claimed in the name of "religion." Translate and you distort; decline to translate and you forfeit standing.
Who has the right to narrate, and to practise?
The contested question is usually not "may outsiders learn" but whether knowledge and responsibility travel together. In context, standing comes from recognition by a particular community and a particular land, and carries lasting obligation; extracting the technique, dropping the obligation and charging by the hour is criticised as turning a relationship into a commodity. On the other side, many communities deliberately open part of their material in order to keep it alive. The workable line usually falls on three questions: who authorised it, who receives the proceeds, and can those described say no.
Why is this, of all subjects, the hardest to count?
Day 18's Bahá'í problem was the gap between rolls and self-report; here the difficulty comes one step earlier — those being counted may not regard themselves as "having a religion" at all. Add single-answer census forms, the wide overlap of traditional practice with Christian identity, and material that may not be disclosed by rule, and any total is an artefact of one particular instrument. The more reliable move is to abandon totals and look at checkable parts: practitioners registered in one region, ceremonies held per year, speakers of a given language.

This page describes Indigenous and shamanic traditions from a neutral, comparative standpoint, aiming to present their internal logic and enormous diversity without praise, disparagement or ranking, and without assessing any living person. "Shaman" is an externally imposed classification, used here for convenience only and not as any tradition's self-description. Population figures are recent estimates that differ enormously depending on the instrument used; the basis of each is stated in the text. Some knowledge is, by the rules of the traditions themselves, not for outside circulation, and this page draws only on published scholarly and official material.
