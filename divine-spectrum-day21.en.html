World Religions

The Spectrum of the Divine

Day 21Comparative~three-quarters of the world claims a religious affiliation

"Do you believe in God?" looks like a neutral question, but it smuggles in a set of assumptions: that there is a thing called God, that it either exists or does not, and that faith is your answer to that yes-or-no. Laid against the world's actual religious landscape, those assumptions buckle. Hinduism, the third-largest tradition, fits neither "monotheist" nor "polytheist." Buddhism and Jainism have no creator god and are unmistakably religions. And most of the 1.9 billion people counted as religiously unaffiliated are not atheists.

"Truth is one; the wise call it by many names."— Rig Veda 1.164.46 (Ekam sad viprā bahudhā vadanti), among the most quoted lines in the Indian traditions

History & Origins

How the question about "God" came to be asked

Across the ancient Near East, the Mediterranean and South Asia, the norm was many gods coexisting: each city, tribe and mountain had its own, and none excluded the others. The mainstream scholarly reconstruction of Israelite religion traces a gradual path — first worshipping only one god (monolatry, which grants that others exist), then insisting that only one god exists at all. An earlier experiment was the pharaoh Akhenaten's exclusive cult of the Aten (c. 14th century BCE), which collapsed almost as soon as he died.

Between roughly 800 and 200 BCE, several civilizational regions began asking abstract questions about ultimate reality at close to the same time: the Upanishads probed Brahman (the ultimate ground of everything), Greek philosophers hunted a first cause, Israelite prophets pushed their God from tribal patron to lord of the cosmos, and Chinese literati theorized Tian (Heaven). Karl Jaspers named this the Axial Age. It is a contested historical hypothesis — the timing is not really synchronous and the causal story is murky — but it makes one point well: reaching for a single encompassing ultimate was a road several civilizations walked separately, not the property of any one of them.

Doubt is just as old. India's Cārvāka school denied the soul and the afterlife; Democritus and Epicurus explained the world by atoms and natural law. Those lines stayed marginal for a long time. Only in 18th-century Europe did deism (a God who made the world and then left it alone) and openly avowed atheism become positions one could sign one's name to.

One further layer is easy to miss: the categories themselves have a history. "Religion" and "theism" are products of 19th-century European comparative scholarship, and they take the Abrahamic traditions as their default shape — one true God, an orthodoxy, an exclusive membership. Measure East Asia's three coexisting teachings or an Indigenous animate world (Days 16 and 19) with that ruler and you mostly get distortion. Max Müller coined henotheism in the 1860s to describe Vedic hymns precisely because the available vocabulary did not fit.

Try to re-sort those figures by concept of the divine and two things collapse immediately.

First: Hinduism will not fit a box. Some 1.2 billion people are filed under one label, but the tradition runs from strict monism (everything is Brahman) to exuberant devotion to many deities. The mono/poly distinction simply does no work here.

Second: "unaffiliated" does not mean atheist. Those 1.9 billion are counted by affiliation, not belief. Pew's 2024 survey of American "nones" found that about 70% still believe in God or some higher power, and 63% believe in spiritual forces beyond the natural world. Roughly two-thirds of the world's unaffiliated live in China — where large numbers of people who report "no religion" on a survey also venerate ancestors, make offerings at temples and consult fengshui. (In the 2018 Chinese General Social Survey about 4% identified as Buddhist, while roughly a third reported believing in the Buddha or bodhisattvas.) Affiliation, belief and practice are three separate things, and none of them answers for the other two.

As for people who call themselves convinced atheists: Gallup International's cross-national polling (2025, 42 countries) puts the figure near 10% globally, with about 14% in high-income countries and 3% in low-income ones. Such polls cover a limited set of countries and rely on self-identification — again, orders of magnitude only.

Core Beliefs

Nine positions on the spectrum

The terms below are scholarly descriptive tools, not a ranking and not developmental stages. Most living traditions occupy several of them at once.

  • Animism — the sacred is distributed across mountains, animals, objects and ancestors, with no center. Common to Indigenous and many folk traditions.
  • Polytheism — many gods with distinct offices and personalities: Greece and Rome, Norse religion, Shinto's "eight million kami," the Yoruba orisha.
  • Henotheism / monolatry — other gods exist, but you serve one. Vedic hymns and early Israelite religion.
  • Monotheism — one God, exclusively. Judaism, Islam, Sikhism, the Bahá'í Faith; Christianity's Trinity is, in its own self-description, one God in three persons rather than three gods.
  • Deism — God made the world and its laws and then withdrew, answering no prayers. The characteristic position of 18th-century Euro-American intellectuals.
  • Panentheism — the world is in God, and God also exceeds the world. Sufi accounts of the unity of being, Kabbalah and Christian process theology all sit near here.
  • Pantheism — God simply is everything there is, with nothing outside. Spinoza is the paradigm; Advaita Vedanta is often read this way, though strictly it is closer to monism.
  • Nontheism — no creator at the center, yet the sacred and an ultimate goal remain. Buddhism does not argue from a creator; Jainism explicitly denies one; Confucianism leaves gods and the afterlife aside while revering Heaven and the ancestors.
  • Atheism / agnosticism — the first holds that no god exists, the second that the matter is unknowable. Cārvāka; modern secular humanism (Day 28).

Why insist it is not a staircase? Because the 19th century circulated an evolutionary sequence — animism to polytheism to monotheism — that conveniently placed the societies proposing it at the summit. The archaeological and textual evidence does not support that single line.

Principles & Practice

Change the concept of God and daily life changes with it

The grammar of prayer

If the divine is personal and responsive, prayer is address and petition — the Psalms, duʿāʾ, the devotional calling of a name. If the ultimate is an impersonal order, "prayer" is closer to tuning yourself to it: Buddhist meditation, the Daoist accord with the Way, the Stoic acceptance of nature. If the sacred is spread among ancestors and local spirits, it is negotiation and upkeep: offerings, vows fulfilled, divination. All three are called prayer; they are not the same act.

The problem of images

Islam and Judaism prohibit images, on the grounds that the infinite cannot be boxed into a finite form; Islamic calligraphy and geometry are the positive yield of that prohibition. Hindu darshan ("seeing") runs the other way: you look at the image and are looked at in return, and that exchange is the worship. Orthodox Christianity takes a middle path — icons may receive veneration but not worship, a distinction hammered out in the iconoclast controversies of the 8th and 9th centuries.

Where ethics comes from

Three sources: commands issued by God (Torah, sharia), a law built into the cosmos (karma, dharma, the Way), and principles people settle among themselves (secular humanism). Notably, all three tend to yield similar rules — do not kill, do not steal, keep faith, protect the weak. The divergence is not in the conclusions but in what grounds them and whom you have wronged when you break them.

Where deliverance points, and the devout without a god

Where God is personal, the endpoint is usually being saved — grace, intercession, judgment. In nontheistic traditions it is liberation by one's own effort, through practice and the unbinding of karma. In animist and ancestral traditions it is keeping the balance so that people, spirits and land each stay in place. And the almsgiving of Theravada laypeople, the austerities of Jain ascetics, the ancestral rites of Confucian households all proceed without an omnipotent creator anywhere in the picture, while sustaining canons, communities, disciplines and life-cycle rites. That is the single largest counterexample to the assumption that a religion must have a god.

One last point that gets underrated: the range of views inside a tradition is often wider than the gap between traditions. Christianity holds both evangelicals committed to a personal God acting in history and liberal theologians who speak of God as the depth of reality; Reconstructionist Judaism openly works with a non-personal conception; Sufi and juridical currents in Islam describe the distance between God and the world quite differently.

Common misreadings

Misreading: religious history evolved from many gods to one.
The evidence does not support that single line. Polytheism and monotheism have alternated across societies, and monotheistic traditions keep generating pantheistic and impersonal formulations from within. The ladder is a 19th-century European construction that placed its authors at the top.

Misreading: Hinduism has 330 million gods, so it is textbook polytheism.
In the phrase "33 koti," koti most plausibly means "kinds" rather than "ten million" — 33 classes of divine being. More importantly, mainstream Vedanta treats the gods as manifestations of one Brahman. A person can hold a monist philosophy and still offer flowers to Ganesha at home; inside the tradition that is not a contradiction.

Misreading: the religiously unaffiliated are atheists.
Affiliation, belief and practice are three different things. Most of the world's 1.9 billion unaffiliated are not convinced atheists; in US surveys about seven in ten still believe in God or a higher power. Cross-national polling puts self-declared convinced atheists at roughly one in ten.

Misreading: without a god it is not a religion.
Buddhism does not argue from a creator, Jainism denies one outright, and Confucianism sets gods and the afterlife aside — yet each has scriptures, community, ethical discipline and rites of passage. Defining religion by belief in God takes the shape of the Abrahamic traditions as a universal template.

Across traditions · the contemporary picture

One word, different semantic fields. Kami, orisha, Tian, Brahman and Allah can all end up translated as "God," but they do not point at the same thing: a sacred force within nature, an unnameable ultimate, a personal party to a covenant. Translation is the first loss — and the most common trap in comparative work.

Shared root, divergent readings. The three Abrahamic faiths share a strict monotheism yet split over whether God can become incarnate (Days 1–7). Within the Indian traditions, theistic bhakti and non-theistic Samkhya coexist under a single dharmic frame (Days 9–12). Which suggests that mono-versus-poly is not the sharpest knife for dividing traditions.

Two contemporary curves running at once. Religious populations are growing across the Global South, driven largely by demography, while the unaffiliated share rises in high-income countries alongside "spiritual but not religious" identities. These run in parallel, not zero-sum — Day 25 takes up that thread.

The interface with science. Pressure from cosmology and evolutionary biology on design arguments has moved some theology toward panentheism and impersonal formulations, and moved other traditions to insist more firmly on separating questions of faith from questions of science. The four models of that relationship wait for Day 27.

Going deeper

Why is "do you believe in God?" a question that barely parses in some cultures?
Because it assumes the core of religion is belief — orthodoxy, holding the right propositions. In East Asia and many folk traditions the core is practice — orthopraxy, doing the right things. What matters is whether the ancestral rites were performed at the right season and whether the etiquette was correct, not which propositions you hold inwardly. Someone who tends the graves every year and burns incense twice a month may genuinely not know how to answer, and may not think an answer is needed. It explains how one person can petition the Buddha, Mazu and the earth god and still write "no religion" on a form (Day 16). It also means the affiliation column in global religious statistics carries the imprint of one particular tradition's concerns.
If theism and atheism reach similar ethical conclusions, what does the concept of God actually change?
Perhaps not what you do but three other things: where the warrant comes from (command, consensus or cosmic law), whom you have wronged when you fail (God, all beings, society, yourself), and how the motivation holds up under pressure — when doing right brings no return and real cost, the different sources supply different amounts of ballast. There is also the framing of meaning and suffering: a personal-God tradition can say there is a plan, a nontheistic one can say this is dependent origination or karma working out, and a secular stance may refuse any ultimate explanation and grant that some suffering is simply pointless. The psychological and social consequences of those three answers remain a live empirical dispute in the sociology of religion.
Does the rise of the unaffiliated mean a sense of the sacred is disappearing?
The data do not support that equation. The places where the unaffiliated grow fastest tend to see astrology, mindfulness, ecological spirituality and healing communities grow alongside — a shift from institutional belonging to personal assembly, not from something to nothing. The open question is whether assembled spirituality, detached from community and intergenerational transmission, can supply what organized religion supplied: support in the face of death, mutual aid that crosses class lines, rituals that turn strangers into companions. That is partly an empirical question and partly a social experiment still under way.
